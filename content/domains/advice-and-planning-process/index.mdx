# Advice and Planning Process

Advice and Planning Process is the analytical work a firm does between learning something about a client and telling that client what to do. It covers how you gather a situation, how you reason from it to a recommendation, what you write down about that reasoning, and how often you go back and check whether the recommendation still holds. A firm that has settled what it believes about markets still has to decide how that belief becomes a specific answer for a specific household, over and over, for years.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up two earlier decisions. [Set Your Advice Approach](/process/set-your-advice-approach) settles the substance: the investment philosophy, whether the firm holds discretionary authority to act without asking each time, and the boundary between advice the firm gives and advice it refers out. [Design the Firm](/process/design-the-firm) settles who the firm serves and what it offers them, which is what any process has to actually fit. This area takes those and builds the working method — the repeatable sequence that produces recommendations and keeps them current — which neither step covers.

## Key ideas

### The method is what repeats, not the answer

A firm gives different advice to different clients, so the thing that has to be consistent is not the recommendation but the route to it. In practice that route has a small number of stages: understand the situation, analyze it against what the client is trying to do, form a recommendation with reasons, implement it, and come back to it later. None of that is exotic, and none of it is optional.

The value of naming the stages is that it makes gaps visible. When advice goes wrong it is usually not because the adviser reasoned badly at the analysis stage; it is because a stage got compressed or dropped when the week was busy, and nobody noticed which one.

### Discovery does not close after onboarding

The obligation to understand a client's situation before recommending anything is not satisfied once by an intake questionnaire. A client's circumstances move — income, dependents, health, an inheritance, a change in what they are actually worried about — and a recommendation reasoned from a situation that no longer exists is not a good recommendation just because it was good when it was made.

Two practical habits keep this honest. Ask what changed at every scheduled contact rather than only when the client volunteers something, and treat a client's stated goals as a thing that can change rather than a fixed input recorded at the start. The adviser who assumes a client's risk tolerance is a permanent trait finds out otherwise during the first market decline the client actually experiences as a client.

A state-registered adviser's equivalent duty comes from state rule, most often modeled on the NASAA unethical business practices rule, and the wording is not identical. Read your own state's version rather than assuming it tracks the federal one.

### A recommendation is a conclusion plus the reasons for it

The recommendation a client hears is short. The reasoning behind it — what you understood about their situation, what you considered, and why you landed where you did — is what makes it defensible later, to the client and to anyone else who asks. Alternatives matter here specifically: a recommendation reached without ever considering a different route is hard to distinguish, afterward, from a default.

Write the reasoning down at the time, in a few sentences, rather than reconstructing it a year later from memory. What form that note takes, where it lives, and how long it is kept belong to [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation); what belongs here is the discipline of producing something worth keeping in the first place.

### Scope shows up in the work, not just in the agreement

Every firm draws a line between what it advises on and what it refers out — tax return preparation, legal drafting, insurance placement, an asset it does not manage. That line is easy to state and hard to hold, because clients do not arrive with questions sorted by professional category. They ask about the thing in front of them.

Holding the line in practice means two things. First, deciding in the moment whether a question sits inside your scope or outside it, rather than answering because you happen to know something. Second, having somewhere to send the client when it sits outside — a referral that goes nowhere is not really a boundary. A firm that quietly answers out-of-scope questions has widened its scope without saying so, and the widened version is what a client will reasonably believe they were promised.

### Revisiting is where most of the advice actually happens

Most of a firm's advice over a client's life is not the first plan. It is the twentieth revision of it. So the review cadence is a real design decision: how often you go back to a client's situation, what you actually look at when you do, and what the firm does about the gap between the plan and what happened.

Alongside the scheduled cycle, decide what forces an unscheduled look. A client's job change, a large inflow or outflow, a change in the firm's own approach, or a market move large enough to have changed the client's position materially are all events that should pull a review forward rather than wait for the calendar. A firm with only a calendar trigger reviews everything on the same day and nothing when it matters.

### Different answers, same method

A process worth having produces genuinely different recommendations for two clients whose situations differ, and produces them by the same route. That is a stricter test than it sounds. If your method reaches the same portfolio and the same advice for nearly everyone, either your clients are more alike than you think or the method is not doing much work between the intake and the answer.

The reverse failure is just as common: advice that varies for reasons that have nothing to do with the client — the adviser's mood, how recently a topic came up at a conference, which client asked more insistently. Running the same stages every time is what keeps variation traceable to the client's situation rather than to yours.

## Common misconceptions

- That a financial plan is the deliverable. The document is a snapshot of the analysis on a particular day; the advice is the ongoing work of keeping it right.
- That gathering more data is the same as understanding a client. A long intake form can collect a great deal and still miss what the client is actually worried about.
- That discretionary authority changes what analysis is required. It changes who acts on the recommendation, not how carefully it has to be reasoned.
- That a repeatable process means a rigid one. The stages repeat; how much work each one takes should differ enormously between a simple situation and a complicated one.
- That explaining the recommendation well is the same as reasoning it well. Explaining it to the client is [Client Education](/domains/client-education); reaching it in the first place is this area's job, and a clear explanation of weak analysis is still weak analysis.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
In a solo firm you are both the person who forms the recommendation and the only person who could catch a flaw in it, so build the check into the method rather than relying on noticing. A short written note per recommendation — what you understood, what you considered, why this — costs a few minutes and is the only version of a second opinion available to you. Re-read the note before the next review with that client: the most useful question at a review is not what the market did, but whether the reasoning you wrote down still describes the client in front of you.
</Callout>
