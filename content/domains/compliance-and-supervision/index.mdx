# Compliance and Supervision

Compliance and Supervision is the standing work of keeping a firm's actual practice lined up with what its policies say, and of watching your own advice when there is no one else in the building to watch it. A firm designs its policies once and then has to keep them describing a real firm, year after year, as clients, services, and the adviser's own habits change. This page is about that ongoing work, not about writing the policies themselves or filing the paperwork that keeps the registration current.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up where two steps leave off. [Write the Policies](/process/write-the-policies) produces a finished, adviser-specific set of documents — a compliance manual, a code of ethics, a privacy policy, a business continuity plan, and a [recordkeeping scheme](/glossary#recordkeeping) — written once, before the firm is approved. [Stay Registered and Compliant](/process/stay-registered-and-compliant) covers the calendar that keeps the registration itself current: the annual amendment, the renewal notice, and client deliveries. This area covers what neither step does: whether the firm's actual practice still matches what those documents describe, and how you check that on your own.

## Key ideas

### The annual review is not the annual amendment

It is easy to treat the yearly [Form ADV](/glossary#form-adv) amendment covered in Stay Registered and Compliant as the whole compliance cycle, but that filing answers a narrower question: whether the registration form is still accurate. A separate, substantive review asks whether the compliance program itself is still doing its job — whether the procedures written into the manual match what the firm actually does, and whether they still address the risks a changing practice actually faces. That review has to happen on a schedule, not only when something goes wrong, and it has to produce something: a dated note of what you checked, what you found, and what you changed as a result. A program that has never been tested against real activity is not evidence of compliance; it is a document nobody has opened since it was filed.

### Supervising yourself without anyone else to check your work

A firm with one adviser still has a supervisory obligation, even though the plain meaning of [supervision](/glossary#supervision) — someone senior reviewing someone junior's work — does not describe a firm of one. The solo version replaces a second person with structure: a written log of your own trades, checked against your [code of ethics](/glossary#code-of-ethics) on a fixed schedule instead of from memory; a periodic sample of your own client files, reviewed the way an examiner would review them rather than the way you remember handling them; and, for some solo advisers, an outside compliance professional engaged periodically for a second, independent read of the program. None of these replace judgment, but each leaves a record that the checking actually happened, which is the part self-supervision most often skips.

### New conflicts and outside activities keep showing up

The code of ethics and the brochure's conflicts section describe the firm as it existed when they were written, and a firm rarely stays still. A referral relationship, an insurance license, a board seat, or a new source of outside compensation can all start well after filing, and each one is a conflict the moment it exists, whether or not the paperwork has caught up. The standing job is noticing a new conflict when it starts, deciding how to manage or disclose it, and updating the record at the next point the guide's disclosure and policy documents call for a change, rather than waiting for a full rewrite to notice it happened.

### Marketing and client communications need ongoing eyes, not a one-time policy

The compliance manual describes a process for reviewing marketing materials before they go out; the standing work is actually running that process against what the firm publishes now. A website update, a new piece of client-facing content, or a [testimonial](/glossary#testimonial) someone else posts about the firm all fall inside the same review the manual describes, whether or not they feel like marketing at the moment they appear. The rules that govern advertising apply to what a prospective client can see today, not to what the manual says should happen, so the review has to keep pace with whatever the firm is actually putting in front of people.

### Watching for drift between the paper and the practice

The gap between what the documents say and what the firm does rarely opens all at once. It opens one small accommodation at a time: a step skipped because a particular week was busy, a workaround adopted because a written procedure turned out to be clumsy, a system swapped out without anyone updating the manual's description of it. Any one of these is minor. Left unexamined for a year or more, they accumulate into a program that describes a firm that no longer exists. Catching drift early, during a scheduled review, keeps the fix small — a paragraph updated, a habit corrected — instead of a documented practice years out of sync with the paper that governs it.

### Supervision changes shape if the firm stops being just you

Everything above assumes the firm is still one person advising and one person checking. If the firm ever adds another adviser, or anyone whose work touches client accounts, supervision stops being a self-review exercise and becomes actual oversight of someone else's work: reviewing their communications, their trading, and their files on a schedule, and being able to show that the review happened. That shift changes what the compliance manual has to say and who is accountable for saying it, but it is a change to plan for rather than a certainty — many solo firms operate for their entire life without making it, and the standing work above is the version they use for as long as that stays true.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: the review described above is required in those terms by Rule 206(4)-7 itself, and the [chief compliance officer](/glossary#chief-compliance-officer) named under Write the Policies is the person who has to see that it happened. A [state-registered adviser](/glossary#state-registered-adviser)'s equivalent obligation comes from state rule instead, and the required frequency and documentation can differ, so confirm your state's version rather than assuming it mirrors the federal rule. The required frequency on either path is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures).
</Callout>

## Common misconceptions

- That satisfying the annual Form ADV amendment described in Stay Registered and Compliant also satisfies the separate obligation to review whether the compliance program itself is working.
- That being the only person in the firm means there is no supervisory obligation, because there is technically no one to supervise.
- That a compliance manual is finished once it is filed, rather than a description that has to keep being tested against what actually happens.
- That marketing review is a single approval step rather than a standing practice that has to keep pace with whatever the firm publishes.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Keep a single, running compliance log rather than scattering review notes across email and memory: the date of each self-review, what you checked, what you found, and what you changed. At [examination](/glossary#examination), that log is what turns "the program is followed" into something you can actually demonstrate, and it is often the one document that most changes how an exam goes for a solo firm.
</Callout>
