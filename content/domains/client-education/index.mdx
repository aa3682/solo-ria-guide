# Client Education

Client Education is the standing job of explaining the firm — its advice, its fees, its process, and what changes along the way — in language a client can actually use. Required disclosure sets a floor for what a client is told; this area is about everything above that floor, delivered continuously rather than once. It is a thinner subject than most of the others in this guide, because it rarely produces its own documents or its own filings. Mostly it shapes how you write and talk about work that other areas of the firm actually govern.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up two earlier decisions. [Design the Firm](/process/design-the-firm) settles who the firm serves, which is the first input into how you explain anything — a client new to investing and a client who spent a career in finance need different things said, even when the underlying facts are identical. [Write the Disclosure Documents](/process/write-the-disclosure-documents) produces the firm's first required, plain-language explanation of itself: the brochure a prospective client reads before signing. This area covers what that document does not — everything you explain to a client, in your own words, outside of what disclosure rules require.

## Key ideas

### Disclosure is the floor, not the whole job

A firm can satisfy every disclosure requirement and still leave a client confused about what they are paying for or what to expect next. The brochure and its supplement are written to a required structure and reviewed for accuracy against that structure; they are not written, and are not meant to be written, as the only conversation a client ever has about the firm. Client Education is what fills the space between "technically disclosed" and "actually understood," and no rule requires that gap to be closed — only the firm's own judgment about what a client needs does.

### Match the explanation to the client, not to a script

The same fact — a fee structure, a discretionary trading authority, a market decline — lands differently depending on what the client already knows. Explaining a concept in the same fixed language to every client, regardless of their background, treats [plain English](/glossary#plain-english) as a box to check rather than a result to achieve. The adviser who serves clients close to a shared life stage still varies the depth of an explanation from one client to the next, because two clients at the same stage rarely arrive with the same starting knowledge.

### Education continues after the client signs

Onboarding is the easiest moment to explain the firm, because a new client expects to be walked through it. The harder and more valuable moments come later: a market decline that tests whether the client actually understood the risk they signed up for, a life change that shifts what the plan needs to cover, or a routine update that a client would otherwise read as more consequential than it is. The adviser who treats education as an onboarding task loses the client's understanding exactly when a change makes it matter most.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: you deliver [Form CRS](/glossary#form-crs), a short, standardized relationship summary written for exactly this purpose, to retail clients, and you update and redeliver it whenever it becomes materially inaccurate. A [state-registered adviser](/glossary#state-registered-adviser) has no equivalent standardized document and relies entirely on the brochure and their own explanation instead.
</Callout>

### The line between educating and marketing

Explaining how a fee works and promoting the firm's services can use the same words, which is exactly why the line between them is easy to blur. Content written to help a client or prospect understand a real concept is education; content written to create demand for the firm is marketing, even when it is dressed as education. Both are legitimate, and a firm does plenty of both, but confusing the two tends to produce material that satisfies neither purpose especially well — too promotional to fully trust, too dry to actually promote anything.

### Format shapes whether an explanation actually lands

A written explanation satisfies a client's own preference to read something on their own schedule; a live conversation surfaces the question a written document never anticipated. Neither replaces the other for anything genuinely material — a fee change or a shift in approach sent only as an email attachment risks being technically delivered and never actually understood. Pair a written explanation of anything material with an actual conversation, even a short one, and use that conversation to confirm the explanation landed rather than assuming a delivered document did the work by itself.

## Common misconceptions

- That a compliant brochure is the same thing as an informed client. A document that satisfies a disclosure rule and a client who actually understands what they read are not guaranteed to be the same outcome.
- That client education is only for clients new to investing. A financially sophisticated client still needs a plain explanation of what is specific to your firm, your process, and your fees — sophistication elsewhere does not transfer automatically.
- That educational content and marketing content are interchangeable so long as the facts in them are accurate. Framing and purpose separate the two even when the underlying claims are identical.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
In a solo firm, the adviser is usually the only person a client hears an explanation from, which means an inconsistency between what you said last year and what you say today is more noticeable, not less. Keep a short, dated note of what you told a client about a material change — a fee shift, a change in approach — separate from the [record of the document you delivered](/domains/records-and-documentation), since the two together are what let you reconstruct, later, what a specific client actually understood and when.
</Callout>
