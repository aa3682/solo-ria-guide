# Marketing and Growth

Marketing and Growth is everything your firm says about itself to people who are not clients yet, and how some of them become clients. Very little of that is free-form. Advertising by an investment adviser is regulated conduct, and the definitions reach further than most people expect — into a website, a newsletter, a talk at a library, and an arrangement where someone is paid to introduce you. This page is about carrying that subject over the life of the firm: what you may claim, which rulebook decides, and what growing actually costs a practice with one adviser in it.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up three earlier pieces of work. [Design the Firm](/process/design-the-firm) settles who the firm serves, which is the entire input into who your marketing is trying to reach — a firm that never named its client cannot describe one to a stranger. [Write the Disclosure Documents](/process/write-the-disclosure-documents) produces the firm brochure, the firm's authoritative account of itself, which everything you later say in public has to agree with. [Leave and Transition Clients](/process/leave-and-transition-clients) produces the book you actually start with, which is both the firm's first revenue and its first source of referrals.

## Key ideas

### What counts as an advertisement is wider than what feels like advertising

Advisers tend to picture advertising as something paid for and placed. The rules are written far more broadly than that: in general, a communication that offers the firm's advisory services to people who are not already clients is an advertisement, whatever you call it and wherever it appears. A website, a monthly letter going to a prospect list, a talk at a community event, a downloadable guide, a social post, an appearance on someone else's show — each can land inside the definition.

So can a piece written purely to explain something, because the classification does not follow your intent. A genuinely educational article that reaches people who are not clients and holds the firm out as an adviser is still an advertisement under the rule. [Client Education](/domains/client-education) covers explaining the firm to the clients you already have; the moment the same explanation is aimed at someone who is not one, it comes back here.

The useful habit is to stop asking whether something feels like marketing and ask two plainer questions instead: does this reach someone who is not a client, and does it offer what the firm sells? Whether a given piece then gets reviewed, approved, and retained before it goes out belongs to [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision) rather than here — this area governs what the piece actually says and who it is meant to reach.

### Which advertising rule binds you is a live question

In May 2026, NASAA — the association of state securities regulators — amended four model rules at once to bring state advertising requirements closer to the federal marketing rule that already governs SEC-registered advisers. The four are the two rules on adviser conduct and the two on recordkeeping, each written twice: once for jurisdictions on the older uniform securities act, once for jurisdictions on the 2002 act. The amendments open a door state rules had largely kept shut, allowing a state-registered adviser to use testimonials, endorsements, and performance figures inside stated conditions.

A model rule is not law. It binds nothing until your own state adopts it, and states adopt on their own schedules, in their own wording, or not at all. Two state-registered advisers can therefore be operating under materially different advertising rules in the same year, and no guide can tell you which one covers you. Read your state's rule as your regulator publishes it, and treat the model as a direction of travel rather than as your requirement. This is worth re-reading annually rather than once, because the answer changes when your state acts, not when NASAA does.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: your advertising is governed by the SEC's marketing rule at 17 CFR 275.206(4)-1, which applies to you now and waits on no state's adoption. The state model rules described above were drafted toward it, so the two paths are converging, but the federal rule is the one that binds you and its conditions are the ones to read.
</Callout>

### The brochure sets the outer edge of what marketing can claim

The firm brochure produced in [Write the Disclosure Documents](/process/write-the-disclosure-documents) is the firm's controlling account of itself: the services it provides, what it charges, the discretion it takes, the conflicts it has. That step owns the document, and this area owns everything else the firm says about itself. The constraint running between them is that the everything else cannot promise more.

A marketing page describing a planning service the brochure does not list, or implying a fee arrangement the brochure does not describe, is not really a marketing problem. It is a disclosure problem, and it surfaces when someone reads the two side by side, which is exactly how an examiner reads them. The cheap discipline runs both ways: when a public claim about services or fees changes, read the brochure's matching item before publishing, and when the brochure changes, re-read the website the same week. The adviser who repositions the firm around a narrower client type updates the website in an afternoon and can carry a stale brochure for months without noticing.

### Testimonials, endorsements, and paid introductions are one subject

A client's public praise, an unaffiliated person's public recommendation, and an arrangement to pay someone for sending prospects your way were once handled as separate topics. The federal marketing rule now treats them as one, and the amended state model rules follow the same structure, because from a regulator's seat they are variants of a single thing: somebody other than the firm telling the public that the firm is worth hiring.

What they share is a disclosure obligation and a paper trail. Whether the speaker is a client, whether they received cash or anything else of value, and what conflict that creates all have to be disclosed to the reader clearly and where they will actually see it — not in a footnote under the quote. A compensated arrangement generally needs a written agreement setting out its scope and terms, and both paths bar paying certain disqualified people at all. There are thresholds below which a written agreement is not required and lookback periods deciding who counts as disqualified; those are figures set by rule and by state, so read [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures) rather than a number you remember.

The quieter version is the arrangement where no money moves. An understanding with a tax preparer or an attorney that each will send the other work is still an interest of yours in the recommendation you make, and the honest treatment is to name it rather than to conclude it does not count because nobody was paid.

### Claims about results are the easiest thing to get wrong

Presenting performance is permitted in some form on both paths, and it is the part of marketing with the least room for improvisation. The recurring failures are always the same: a set of accounts or a stretch of time chosen because it flattered, results shown before fees without equal prominence for results after fees, a comparison against a benchmark that is not comparable, and a claim you cannot substantiate when someone asks. A firm that advertises performance also has to be able to produce the working papers behind the figure, which is much of what the state recordkeeping rules amended alongside the conduct rules are about.

Two things are worth settling before any number goes out. The first is whether you advertise performance at all — a firm selling planning and ongoing management can market entirely on what it does for a client and never publish a return, and many deliberately do, because the substantiation burden never expires. The second is that an illustration or a projection is not a track record and cannot be dressed as one. The periods that must be shown and the treatment of hypothetical figures are set by rule and belong on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures).

### Growth costs money or hours, and a solo firm is short of hours

The channels available to a small firm are few and well known: referrals from existing clients, referrals from other professionals, content the firm publishes, a specialty narrow enough to make the firm findable, speaking and local presence, and paid placement. What separates them is not effectiveness in the abstract but what each one draws down. In a firm of one, the scarce input is the adviser's own week, which makes a channel that costs money and little time frequently cheaper than a channel that costs nothing but a day every week forever. Judge each by the clients it actually added over a period long enough to mean something. The adviser who has published weekly for two years and can attribute three clients to it is not running a cheap channel, whatever the invoice says.

The second half of the subject is knowing when to stop. Adding clients faster than the service model can absorb them degrades the service that generated the referrals in the first place, and a solo firm has no bench to absorb the overflow. What the firm can carry at its current price and service design is decided in [Service and Pricing Models](/domains/service-and-pricing-models), and what a new client is worth after the cost of acquiring them is [Firm Economics](/domains/firm-economics). The decision that belongs here is the one most firms never write down: the point at which marketing gets turned off, and what would have to change before it is turned back on.

## Common misconceptions

- That the advertising rules apply to paid advertising and not to a website, a newsletter, or a talk. The definitions turn on who a communication reaches and what it offers, not on whether anyone was billed to place it.
- That because NASAA has modernized its model advertising rules, testimonials are now open to every state-registered adviser. A model rule changes nothing in a state until that state adopts it, in its own wording and on its own timetable.
- That content written to educate is outside the rules because it was not written to sell. Intent does not decide the classification; the audience and the content do.
- That an unpaid referral arrangement creates no conflict because no money changed hands. An expectation of introductions coming back the other way is an interest in the recommendation.
- That a channel costing nothing is free. In a firm of one, the adviser's own hours are the budget that runs out first.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Keep a dated archive of what the firm publishes as the public actually saw it — the page as it read that day, not the current version of the same page. In a solo firm the website gets edited whenever the adviser has a free hour, and there is nobody else who remembers what it said last spring. Keep the substantiation with the claim rather than in your head, so a "we specialize in" line or a published figure carries its support where you will find it a year later under someone else's questioning. Where the review of that material sits in your written procedures is [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision); what this area needs is that the claim and its basis were both recorded at the time.
</Callout>
