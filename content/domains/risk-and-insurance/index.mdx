# Risk and Insurance

Risk and Insurance is about the ways an independent firm can lose money, clients, or its ability to operate, and what it does about each one. Some you avoid by not taking them. Some you absorb, because transferring them costs more than the loss would. Some you pay someone else to carry, and a few you must cover whether you want to or not. This page is about keeping that set of decisions current for as long as the firm exists, rather than settling it once at launch and renewing out of habit.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up three earlier pieces of work. [Decide If Independence Fits](/process/decide-if-independence-fits) is where the trade gets named: risks your current firm quietly absorbs for you become yours, along with the cost of covering them. [Form the Business](/process/form-the-business) creates the legal entity, the firm's first layer of protection and the one most often mistaken for the whole answer. [Write the Policies](/process/write-the-policies) produces the [business continuity and succession plan](/glossary#business-continuity-and-succession-plan), which is how the firm handles the one risk no policy pays out on.

## Key ideas

### Start from the risks, not from the policies

It is tempting to treat this subject as a shopping list, because coverage is the part with a price tag on it. The more useful order runs the other way: write down what could actually go wrong, then decide what to do about each item. A short, honest list usually includes a client claiming your advice caused a loss, a mistake in execution such as a trade or a bill placed wrong, a breach or loss of client data, an [examination](/glossary#examination) that finds something, a dispute with a former employer over your departure, and you being unable to work.

Each gets a different answer. Some are handled by a written procedure, some by keeping enough cash on hand, and only a few by buying coverage. Knowing which is which is what stops a firm from being over-insured and badly exposed at once.

### What the entity protects, and what it does not

A limited liability entity separates the firm's obligations from your personal assets, which is real protection against the firm's contracts, leases, and debts. It is much weaker against the risk this business actually runs: a claim that the advice was negligent generally reaches the person who gave it, and a firm of one has no meaningful gap between the entity and the adviser. That is not an argument against the entity, only against treating it as coverage — it handles one category well and the category that generates most claims against advisers barely at all.

### Read a professional liability policy at its edges

[Errors and omissions coverage](/glossary#errors-and-omissions-coverage) — professional liability insurance for advice — is the policy most independent firms carry, and what decides whether it helps is rarely the headline limit. It is usually written on a claims-made basis, responding to claims reported while the policy is in force rather than to advice given while it was. That makes two details load-bearing: the [retroactive date](/glossary#retroactive-date), which is how far back into your past work the policy reaches, and what happens when you stop carrying it, since a claim can arrive years after the work.

Then read the rest of the edges. Whether defense costs come out of the limit or sit outside it changes what the limit is worth. Exclusions decide whether work you actually do is covered at all — outside business activities, insurance or tax work, and advice on assets you do not manage are common places a policy stops. Data-breach exposure is often not carried by such a policy at all, and where a firm wants it covered it is usually a separate line with its own terms.

### Bonding and net worth are a condition of registration, not insurance

Several states require a state-registered adviser to maintain a [minimum net worth](/glossary#minimum-net-worth), to post a surety bond, or both, usually keyed to whether the firm has custody of client assets, holds discretionary authority, or accepts fees paid substantially in advance. A [surety bond](/glossary#surety-bond) is not coverage for the firm: it is a promise to a third party, backed by an insurer, that pays a client if the firm cannot — and the surety expects the firm to repay it afterward.

Two things here are easy to get wrong. The amounts, the triggers, and whether a bond is required at all are set state by state, so those figures belong on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). And falling below a required net worth is usually itself a reportable event on a short deadline, not a problem to fix quietly first; that deadline is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures) as well. The adviser who watches the firm's net worth drift toward a state minimum treats it as a filing question as much as a cash question.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: net worth and bonding requirements of this kind are conditions of state registration and generally do not reach an adviser the [SEC](/glossary#securities-and-exchange-commission) registers, which imposes no equivalent net capital or bonding requirement. Confirm what the states you notice-file in ask of you rather than assuming nothing applies.
</Callout>

### The risks no policy carries

The largest single exposure in a solo firm is the adviser. If you cannot work, for a season or permanently, clients still hold accounts that need attention, and no professional liability policy addresses that. The firm's answer is the continuity and succession plan written in [Write the Policies](/process/write-the-policies), kept current: a named person, an arrangement they have agreed to, and enough access for them to find client records and act. Personal coverage such as disability or life insurance sits alongside it, protecting your household rather than the firm.

Two other uninsurable risks deserve the same treatment. Concentration — a large share of revenue resting on a few relationships — is managed by whom the firm takes on. Reputation is managed by how the firm behaves when something goes wrong. [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision) covers whether the firm's practice still matches its documents; this area covers what the firm stands to lose when it does not.

### Coverage is a standing review, not a purchase

Assets grow, services get added, a firm takes on discretion it did not have — and each of those can move it across a bonding or net worth line, or outside what its policy covers, without anything visibly changing. So review the whole set on a fixed schedule rather than at renewal, when the only question in front of you is price: what changed about the firm this year, whether the exclusions still describe work you do not do, and whether the state requirements you checked at registration still read the same way. What all of it costs, and how it fits the budget, belongs to [Firm Economics](/domains/firm-economics); what the firm is exposed to is this area's question.

## Common misconceptions

- That forming an entity removes the need for professional liability coverage. They address different risks, and the one an advisory firm most often faces is the one the entity handles worst.
- That a surety bond protects the firm. It protects clients and satisfies a regulator; the firm generally repays what the surety pays out.
- That a [claims-made policy](/glossary#claims-made-policy) covers everything that happened while you were paying for it. What matters is when a claim is reported and how far back the retroactive date reaches.
- That being small keeps a firm out of trouble. Examination findings, breaches, and departure disputes are not proportional to firm size, and a solo firm has no one else to absorb them.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Keep the whole risk picture on one page — coverage in force with its limits, retroactive date, and renewal; the firm's net worth against whatever your state requires; the bond if you carry one; and the named person in your continuity plan with the date they last confirmed it. In a solo firm the danger is rarely a wrong decision; it is a decision made once and never looked at again while the firm around it changed. Date the page each time you review it and keep the superseded versions, since the question later is what was true on a given day.
</Callout>

- [NASAA: Minimum Financial Requirements for Investment Advisers, Model Rule 202(d)-1](https://www.nasaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/07/IA-Model-Rule-Minimum-Financial-Requirements.pdf) — the model rule most states draw on for adviser net worth requirements and for the notice owed when a firm falls below one.
- [NASAA: Bonding Requirements for Investment Advisers, Model Rule 202(e)-1](https://www.nasaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/07/IA-Model-Rule-Bonding.pdf) — the model rule behind state surety bond requirements for advisers with custody or discretionary authority.
- [U.S. Code: Investment Advisers Act section 222](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section80b-18a&num=0&edition=prelim) — the statute limiting a state from imposing higher net capital or bonding requirements on an adviser whose principal office is in another state.
- [NAIC: Insurance Departments](https://content.naic.org/state-insurance-departments) — a directory of every state insurance regulator, which is where questions about a surety or an insurer licensed in your state are answered.
