# Service and Pricing Models

Service and Pricing Models covers two questions that never fully close: what your firm actually offers, and what it charges for offering it. A new firm has to answer both once just to open its doors, but neither answer holds still once clients, capacity, and the market around you keep changing. This page is about how to keep making that decision well, for as long as the firm operates, not how to make it correctly the first time.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up two earlier decisions. [Decide If Independence Fits](/process/decide-if-independence-fits) asks you to estimate whether your book can support a firm on its own, an estimate that only works once you have a rough sense of what you would actually charge. [Design the Firm](/process/design-the-firm) settles who you serve and what you offer them, in plain language, without pricing attached. This area takes both and turns them into a running pricing structure, then keeps adjusting that structure after the firm exists, which neither step covers.

## Key ideas

### How you get paid

Independent advisers charge in a handful of common ways: a percentage of assets under management, a flat retainer, an hourly rate, a fee tied to a specific project, or a recurring subscription unrelated to account size. None of these is required by the fiduciary duty investment advisers already owe their clients, and none is barred by it either — each is compatible, and each nudges your incentives in a different direction. A percentage of assets rewards you for managing more money, but not, on its own, for planning work that never touches an account balance. A subscription decouples revenue from asset levels, which fits a firm serving clients with irregular or modest portfolios. Most firms end up combining more than one, and the combination belongs together with the service design from Design the Firm, not bolted on afterward.

### Price what you actually deliver

Your price should describe your actual service, not an industry average. If your service is ongoing planning plus investment management, a fee that only reflects the assets you manage prices half the work and leaves the rest paid, informally, out of your own time. If your service is a single planning engagement with no ongoing management, a fee sized as though the relationship will run for years overcharges for a one-time deliverable. Working backward from what a given kind of client actually costs you in hours each year produces a more defensible number than starting from a competitor's rate card and adjusting slightly.

### The fee structure is itself a conflict of interest

Every fee structure creates an incentive, and pricing well means being honest with yourself about which one you have built. An assets-based fee can discourage advice to pay down debt or spend down savings, because either shrinks the base the fee is charged against. An hourly fee can discourage efficiency, since time worked is what gets paid. Neither incentive disqualifies the structure, but each has to be disclosed in plain language, and disclosing it honestly is easier when you have already named it to yourself rather than discovering it while [drafting the firm's disclosure documents](/process/write-the-disclosure-documents).

### Segmentation and minimums decide who you can serve

Pricing does not only set what a client pays — it sets which clients you can take on at all. A minimum fee or a minimum account size screens out relationships that would cost more in time than they return in revenue, but it also screens out people you might otherwise want to help. A tiered structure, where service and price both scale with a client's complexity, lets you serve a wider range without the largest relationships quietly subsidizing the smallest. There is no single correct minimum. There is only the minimum that matches the capacity and service model you actually built, not the one that sounds most accessible.

### Pricing is a standing decision, not a settled one

A price set at launch reflects what you knew at launch: your costs, your competitors, and your own uncertainty about what the work was worth. None of that holds still. The adviser reprices annually, comparing the revenue and hours each service tier actually consumed against what was assumed when it was set, and has in different years raised the retainer for new clients while grandfathering the existing book, and folded a once-separate planning fee into the ongoing fee once it became clear clients experienced the two as one service. Revisiting price on a fixed schedule, rather than only once costs become uncomfortable, keeps the adjustment a deliberate choice instead of a delayed reaction.

## Common misconceptions

- That a percentage-of-assets fee is the only professional way to charge, or that clients expect it by default. Flat and subscription pricing are increasingly common and no less legitimate.
- That a lower fee is inherently more ethical. A fee that doesn't cover the real cost of serving a client well isn't more client-friendly — it fails later, after the relationship is already built, instead of failing to start at all.
- That once a fee schedule is in the brochure, changing it is a compliance problem best avoided. Updating a fee schedule is routine; the compliance work is disclosing and documenting the change, not steering around it.
- That pricing is a marketing decision, separate from the service itself. Clients notice when what they were told they're buying doesn't match what the fee structure actually rewards.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
A solo firm feels every pricing mistake directly, because there is no colleague absorbing an underpriced relationship elsewhere in the book. Track hours by service tier for at least a full cycle before concluding a price is wrong — one busy quarter is not evidence. When you do reprice, decide in advance whether the new terms apply to new clients only or to the existing book too, and put that decision in writing before you tell anyone, since the two approaches lead to very different conversations.
</Callout>

- [SEC: Form ADV Part 2 Instructions](https://www.sec.gov/about/forms/formadv-instructions.pdf) — the instructions for Item 5 of the firm brochure, which sets what a fee schedule must disclose, including fee type, billing method, and refund terms.
