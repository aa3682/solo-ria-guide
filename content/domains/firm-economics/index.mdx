# Firm Economics

Firm Economics is the standing question of whether the business works as a business. Revenue arrives, a set of obligations consumes it, and whatever survives that is what pays the person who owns the firm. This page is about keeping that arithmetic honest over years — what the money has to cover, how the shape of the cost base behaves when revenue moves, and how much cash the firm has to hold to absorb a bad stretch without a forced decision.

## Where this starts in the process

If you are working through the guide in order, this area picks up three earlier pieces of work. [Decide If Independence Fits](/process/decide-if-independence-fits) produces the runway estimate: a one-time projection of whether a practice could support you at all, made before the firm exists. [Design the Firm](/process/design-the-firm) settles what the firm offers and how it is staffed, which is what most of the cost base follows from. [Form the Business](/process/form-the-business) creates the entity and its tax treatment, which decide how money legally moves from the firm to you. This area is what happens to all three once revenue is actually arriving.

## Key ideas

### What the revenue has to cover

[Service and Pricing Models](/domains/service-and-pricing-models) owns how the firm charges and what it charges for; this area owns what the resulting revenue has to cover. Those are different questions, and a firm can get the first right and still fail the second.

The list is longer than most new owners expect, and most of it recurs. Registration and filing costs return every year, through the electronic system advisers file in and through whichever regulator registers the firm; the amounts are set by the system operator and by regulators and are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). Technology recurs monthly. Professional liability coverage and any bonding recur annually. Outside help — an accountant, counsel, a compliance professional engaged periodically — recurs irregularly but reliably. Then there is the largest item, which is you: the firm has to pay its owner.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: the recurring registration cost has a different shape. You do not renew a home-state firm registration, but you owe a notice filing fee in each state where your business is large enough to require one, and that list grows as clients spread geographically; what each state charges and how much business triggers a notice filing are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). Treat it as a cost that tracks where your clients live rather than as a fixed annual line.
</Callout>

### Paying yourself is a decision, not a residual

In a firm with one owner it is tempting to treat whatever is in the account as compensation, and to discover the firm's actual profitability only at tax time. The alternative is to decide, in advance, what the firm pays you on a regular schedule, and to treat anything above that as the firm's profit rather than as spending money.

The entity form and the tax election made at formation determine the mechanics — whether you take a draw, a salary, a distribution, or some combination — and that question belongs to your own tax professional, not to a guide. What belongs here is the habit that survives any of those answers: money for taxes comes out of revenue before you consider what is left yours. Nobody withholds on the owner of a firm, and the obligation arrives during the year rather than at the end of it.

### Fixed and variable costs behave differently when revenue moves

Firms whose revenue is a percentage of client assets have revenue that moves with markets. Very little of the cost base moves with it. A firm that loses a fifth of its revenue in a market decline generally still owes the same technology costs, the same coverage, the same registration renewals, and still has to pay its owner something. That asymmetry is the single most important structural fact about a small advisory firm's economics.

Two responses are available and both are ordinary. One is to hold more cash against the swing. The other is to build a cost base with less fixed weight in it — paying for outside help by engagement rather than by retainer, choosing arrangements you can step out of at a year's end. Neither removes the asymmetry; both make a bad year survivable rather than decisive.

### The runway question does not close at launch

The pre-launch runway estimate answered one question: could you get from resigning to a working firm without running out. The ongoing version asks something narrower and more useful — how many months the firm could operate at current costs if revenue stopped improving, or fell.

Keep it as a live number rather than a memory of the number you produced before the firm existed. Recalculate it when anything structural changes: a cost added, a large client leaving, a price change taking effect, a year where markets moved the fee base a long way. The adviser who checks it once a year and after any event that moved revenue materially usually finds it drifting slowly rather than breaking suddenly, which is exactly the point of looking.

### The balance sheet is a management document

[Risk and Insurance](/domains/risk-and-insurance) treats net worth strictly as a regulatory threshold — a line some states require an adviser to stay above, with a filing obligation attached to falling below it. This area treats the same statement as a management tool, which is a different use of the same numbers.

Read as management, the balance sheet answers questions the income statement cannot. How much of the cash on hand is actually the firm's, and how much is tax not yet remitted or fees collected for a period not yet worked? Are receivables aging, and from whom? Does the firm carry debt, and what does servicing it cost in a year when revenue falls? A firm can be profitable on paper for several quarters and still be running out of money, and the balance sheet is where that shows up first.

### Growth changes the economics, not only the revenue

More clients is not automatically better economics. Every additional relationship consumes time, and past some point the firm buys capacity — help, better systems, or a second person — which converts a variable constraint into a fixed cost. Whether growth improves the firm depends on whether revenue per relationship rises faster than the cost of serving one.

That makes a few numbers worth tracking over time rather than at a moment: revenue per client, the share of revenue that recurs without new work each year, and how concentrated revenue is across the book. Pricing decisions that follow from any of this belong to [Service and Pricing Models](/domains/service-and-pricing-models); what belongs here is noticing that a firm which has grown steadily for three years can still be earning its owner less per hour than it did at the start.

## Common misconceptions

- That revenue growth and firm health are the same thing. A firm can add clients every year and see its economics deteriorate the whole time.
- That the owner is paid out of what is left over. Owner compensation is a cost the firm has to be able to cover, and treating it as a residual hides a firm that is not actually working.
- That a good year means the cost base can grow to match it. Costs added in a strong year are usually fixed, and the revenue that justified them usually is not.
- That the runway estimate made before launch answers the question permanently. It answered a starting question, under assumptions the operating firm has already outgrown.
- That profitability and solvency are the same. A profitable firm that has spent money it owes in tax is still a firm that cannot pay the tax.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Pay yourself on a schedule you set in advance rather than when the balance looks comfortable — in a solo firm nothing except that habit distinguishes the firm's cash from your own. Set aside money for tax as revenue arrives rather than at a deadline. Once a quarter, look at three numbers together: what the firm earned, what it holds, and how many months it could operate on that. Any one of them alone can look fine while the set of them is telling you something.
</Callout>
