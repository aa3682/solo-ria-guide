# File and Get Approved

Every step before this one produced something to file: a formed entity, a regulator determination, a qualified representative, a disclosure set, and a policy manual. This step is where all of it actually goes in. You request access to the filing system, submit the firm's and your own forms as a pair, pay what is owed, and then sit through a review the regulator controls, not you. Filing is mostly mechanical. Getting approved depends on how well the earlier steps were done.

## Why it matters

Nothing from the earlier steps takes effect on its own. Until the regulator approves the registration, you cannot hold the firm out as a [registered investment adviser](/glossary#registered-investment-adviser), open an account for a client, or charge an advisory fee. A reviewer reads the brochure against the form and the form against your individual filing, and this is the first point where anyone outside the firm tests whether those documents actually agree with each other.

Most of what goes wrong here costs time rather than killing the application outright. A missing exhibit, an inconsistent answer, or a fee that does not match the filing usually draws a request to fix it, not a denial. But the filing sits idle until you respond, and the response goes back into a queue behind other work. If you are timing your departure from a current firm, or already have clients waiting, a filing that bounces even once can cost weeks you had not planned to lose.

## What actually happens

### Get entitled to the filing system

Before anything can be submitted, someone at the firm needs entitled access to [IARD](/glossary#iard), the system both state-registered and SEC-registered advisers file through, operated by [FINRA](/glossary#finra) on behalf of the regulators that use it. Getting entitled means requesting an account, naming who at the firm may log in and submit filings, and verifying that person's identity. In a solo firm, that person is you.

Start this early. Account setup and identity verification run on the system operator's timeline, not yours, and there is no reason to let it sit on the critical path when nothing else about the filing depends on it.

### Submit the firm's and the individual's filings together

The firm and its representative file separately but move as a pair. The firm's [Form ADV](/glossary#form-adv) carries the disclosure documents you wrote in [Write the Disclosure Documents](/process/write-the-disclosure-documents). Your own [Form U4](/glossary#form-u4) carries the qualification record you built in [Qualify as an Adviser](/process/qualify-as-an-adviser). An approved firm with no approved representative cannot yet do business, and neither can the reverse, so treat the two applications as one submission and keep them moving on the same timeline.

Before you submit, read the two filings against each other the way a reviewer will: the services described in the brochure should match what the form says the firm does, and your disclosure answers on the individual filing should not contradict anything the firm discloses about itself.

### Pay what is owed

Filing triggers more than one fee: a system fee for the filing itself, a registration fee to the regulator, and often a separate fee tied to your individual filing. None of the amounts belong on this page, since they are set by the system operator and by regulators and change over time; [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures) holds each one with its source. What belongs here is the mechanics: fund the account before you submit, because an application with an unpaid fee does not move forward, and a shortfall discovered mid-review adds a delay you could have avoided.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: you do not pay your home state a registration fee for the firm, but you likely owe [notice filing](/glossary#notice-filing) fees in every state where you do enough business to require one, on top of the system and SEC fees; those fees and the level of business that triggers a notice filing are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). Confirm the list of states before you fund the account, since it is easy to miss one.
</Callout>

### Respond to comments and deficiency letters

A regulator reviewing the filing may come back with a comment or a [deficiency letter](/glossary#deficiency-letter): a missing exhibit, a question about an inconsistent answer, or a request to revise a section of the brochure. Read each request carefully and answer exactly what was asked. If you disagree with a comment, explain the discrepancy in writing rather than quietly resubmitting something unchanged.

Treat the response deadline as real. An application that sits past the time the regulator allows for a response can be treated as abandoned, which starts the whole filing over rather than merely delaying it. Keep a copy of every comment and every response; the exchange becomes part of the record an examiner can ask to see later, in [Stay Registered and Compliant](/process/stay-registered-and-compliant).

### Know when the registration is effective

Registration is effective on a specific date, not on the day you submitted or the day you stopped hearing from the reviewer. Confirm that date directly in the filing system rather than assuming silence means approval, and keep a copy of the confirmation with the rest of the firm's formation records.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: [section 203(c) of the Investment Advisers Act](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section80b-3&num=0&edition=prelim) gives the Commission a deadline to act on an application for registration — by that point it either grants the registration by order or institutes proceedings to deny it — rather than a date on which your own registration takes effect by itself. Once proceedings are instituted, a separate deadline for concluding proceedings applies and can be extended, so a filing that draws them resolves well after the deadline to act has passed; a state's review runs on a different, separate clock. Both the deadline to act on an application and the deadline for concluding proceedings are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures).
</Callout>

By the end of this step you hold a firm that is actually registered: an effective date, a confirmed status for both the firm and your individual filing, and a paper trail of every fee paid and every comment answered along the way. Only from this point can the firm hold itself out to the public and take on its first client.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Do you know who at the firm holds entitled access to the filing system, and is that access already set up rather than something you will request when you need it?
- Have you read the brochure and the form side by side for anything that does not match?
- Is the filing account funded for every fee the submission will trigger, including any that apply only in your situation?
- If a deficiency letter arrived tomorrow, do you know how much time you would have to respond, and where that deadline is written down?
- Have you confirmed the actual effective date in the filing system, rather than assuming approval once you stop hearing back?

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until the filing is ready to request entitled access, and losing time to account setup that could have run in parallel with earlier steps.
- Submitting the firm's and the individual's filings on different timelines, so one sits approved while the other is still incomplete.
- Letting the filing account run short on funds partway through review, which stalls an otherwise clean application.
- Missing a deficiency response deadline and having the application treated as abandoned rather than merely delayed.
- Treating the absence of new comments as approval, instead of confirming the actual effective date.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision): the reviewer is testing the policies and disclosures that area covers, and any gap it finds surfaces here first.
- [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation): the filing, every comment, and every response become part of the record this step starts building for the life of the firm.
- [Risk and Insurance](/domains/risk-and-insurance): some states condition approval on proof of the bonding or insurance the firm's risk plan calls for, so that plan has to be in place before the filing can clear.

<Callout type="info">
Log every filing event as it happens: the [entitlement](/glossary#entitlement) request, each submission, each fee paid, each comment received and answered, and the confirmed effective date. An examiner may later ask not just whether the firm is registered but how it got there, and a solo firm has no one else's memory to fall back on.
</Callout>
