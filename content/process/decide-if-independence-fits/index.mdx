# Decide If Independence Fits

Before you touch a business plan, a regulator, or a client conversation, there is a decision to make: whether to leave the firm you are at now and build an independent [registered investment adviser](/glossary#registered-investment-adviser) (RIA) firm, or stay where you are. This step does not produce paperwork or a filing. It produces a decision, made with your eyes open, about whether the independent model actually fits your situation.

To make this concrete, the rest of this guide follows one recurring example: the adviser. The adviser is a hypothetical [investment adviser representative](/glossary#investment-adviser-representative) currently working at a broker-dealer, with a book of about 80 clients and roughly $300,000 in annual revenue — illustrative, round numbers used throughout this guide, not a target or a benchmark. The adviser's assets under management fall below the threshold that would require [SEC](/glossary#securities-and-exchange-commission) registration (see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures)), so the adviser's path runs through state registration, the guide's default scenario. Later steps follow the adviser through each stage of building an independent firm.

## Why it matters

Every step after this one assumes you have already decided to go independent. Design the Firm assumes you are building something. Form the Business assumes you are ready to create a legal entity. The steps on registration assume you intend to file.

If you make this decision quickly, or without looking closely at your own numbers, the gap tends to show up later — after you have spent money on formation, insurance, and technology, or after you have resigned from your current firm and started the clock on moving clients. Deciding well here does not guarantee a smooth path through the rest of the guide, but deciding poorly here makes every later step harder.

The decision really has two separate parts. One is a numbers question: across a plausible range of outcomes, can your practice generate enough revenue to support you? The other is a preference question: do you actually want to run a business, with everything that comes with owning one, or do you mainly want more say inside a business someone else runs? People sometimes answer only one of these and skip the other, and either gap tends to surface at an inconvenient point later in the process.

## What actually happens

### Take stock of what you have

Start with your own numbers, not industry averages. How many clients do you actually serve, what does each one generate in revenue, and how much of that revenue recurs automatically versus depends on you doing something new each year? A book concentrated in a handful of large relationships behaves differently from one spread across many smaller ones if even a few of those relationships do not come with you.

Whether clients would follow you, and what you are and are not permitted to say to them before you leave, is a real question — but it is the subject of its own step later in the guide, [Leave and Transition Clients](/process/leave-and-transition-clients), not something to work out here. At this stage you are only estimating, honestly, how portable your current book is likely to be.

### Weigh what changes

Independence moves a set of responsibilities that currently sit with your firm onto you directly: where client assets are custodied, who oversees your compliance program, which systems you run the practice on, how you find new clients, and how you insure the business against the risk of a client dispute or a mistake. In exchange, you generally gain more control over what you charge, what you recommend, and how you run the practice day to day. Neither side of that trade is free, and it helps to write out, specifically, which pieces of control you actually want and which pieces of support you would miss.

### Estimate what it costs to get there

Build a rough runway estimate: the one-time cost of forming the entity and setting up initial compliance and technology, ongoing costs like insurance and regulatory fees, and a stretch of time where revenue from the new firm is still ramping up while some expenses stay fixed. None of the specific fee amounts belong on this page — they change over time and by state, and they live on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures) instead. What belongs here is the shape of the estimate: for illustration, if the adviser assumed a six-month gap between leaving the old firm and reaching a comparable income at the new one, the runway estimate would need to cover six months of both personal and business expenses, not just one or the other.

### Write down where you land

This step produces a written decision, not just a feeling. Put it in writing: a go or no-go call, your rough runway estimate in months, and the open questions you are carrying into later steps — [which regulator you will register with](/process/choose-your-regulator), what kind of entity you will form, how you plan to approach current clients. You do not need firm answers to those open questions yet. You need them written down somewhere you will actually look at again once the next steps ask you to resolve them.

## Questions to ask yourself

- How much of your current revenue is recurring, and how much depends on transactions or activity you would have to repeat each year?
- If a meaningful share of your current clients did not come with you, would the remaining book still support the firm you are picturing?
- Can you and your household absorb a stretch of reduced income, and for roughly how long?
- Do you want to spend part of most weeks on the administrative and compliance work of owning a business, not just on client-facing work?
- What would have to be true for you to conclude, later, that this was the wrong decision — and would you actually notice if it happened?

## Common mistakes

- Treating the runway estimate as a single number instead of a range, then getting caught out when the slower end of the range turns out to be the real one.
- Assuming, without checking, that most or all current clients will follow. Client relationships are not automatically portable, and what you can say to clients before you leave your current firm is often more restricted than people expect.
- Deciding based mainly on the appeal of more control, without running the numbers on whether the resulting practice can actually cover its own costs.
- Treating this decision as reversible once later steps are underway. Unwinding a registration filing or a client transition already in motion costs far more than pausing here would.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Firm Economics](/domains/firm-economics): The runway estimate and revenue analysis in this step draw directly on how an independent firm's economics work over time.
- [Service and Pricing Models](/domains/service-and-pricing-models): How your current book could translate into a pricing model of your own design affects how realistic your revenue estimate is.
- [Risk and Insurance](/domains/risk-and-insurance): Weighing what changes includes taking on risks, and the cost of insuring against them, that your current firm currently absorbs for you.

<Callout type="info">
At this step you are not yet doing anything with clients, and that is deliberate — premature outreach can conflict with your current firm's agreements and with regulatory expectations about solicitation before you have actually left. Keep your analysis in your own records rather than on your current firm's systems. Document the numbers you used for [your runway estimate](/tools/reaching-your-written-decision), the assumptions behind them, and your written go or no-go decision, since later steps will ask you to revisit each of them.
</Callout>
