# Leave and Transition Clients

By this point the new firm exists, is registered, and has a working relationship with a [qualified custodian](/glossary#qualified-custodian) and the operational systems to run an account. What it does not have yet is a single client. This step covers the mechanics of actually leaving your current firm and moving the clients who choose to follow you: resigning cleanly, sorting out what you can and cannot take with you, getting each client's real consent, and moving their accounts to the new custodian.

## Why it matters

Nearly everything in this step touches something someone else has a legal claim over: your current firm's records and client relationships, the prior firm's view of why you left, and each client's own right to decide, freely, whether to follow you. Getting any of these wrong does not just create friction. A mishandled departure can trigger a claim from your prior firm, a disputed entry on your own regulatory record, or a client transition that regulators or your prior firm can credibly say was not the client's free choice.

The step is also the hinge in the sequence: the work of resigning, telling clients you are open, and moving real accounts is where a firm on paper turns into a firm in practice.

## What actually happens

### Resign from the prior firm

Timing your resignation is a judgment call shaped by your employment agreement, any [restrictive covenants](/glossary#restrictive-covenant) in it, and whether your current firm participates in a recruiting protocol — an arrangement some firms join that lets a departing representative take specific, limited client information without triggering a claim under agreements they would otherwise be bound by. Whether your firm belongs to one, and what it actually permits, is a fact to confirm directly; a firm outside one leaves you bound by your own agreement, with none of a protocol's carve-outs available.

Once you resign, your current firm files [Form U5](/glossary#form-u5) documenting your departure, including any disclosures about how the relationship ended. That filing becomes part of your own regulatory record, visible to regulators and, in redacted form, the public, for as long as you remain registered — see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#leave-and-transition-clients) for how long your firm has to file it. The firm controls that narrative, so read it once it posts and know how to dispute it if it misstates what happened.

### Determine what you can take — and what you can't

Client contact information, account numbers, and performance history are three things departing advisers most want to bring along, and three things most likely to be restricted by your employment agreement, your firm's policies, trade-secret law, or privacy rules that protect the client rather than either firm. A [recruiting protocol](/glossary#recruiting-protocol), where one applies, changes this analysis without resolving it — protocols typically cover a narrow slice of information and leave the rest governed by whatever agreement you signed.

This is a fact-specific legal question, not a rule this guide can state for you: what you can lawfully take depends on your own agreements, your firm's policies, and your state's law. Get counsel on your actual situation before copying a single file, rather than relying on what a colleague did elsewhere.

### Notify clients and secure their consent

A client does not move to the new firm just because you moved. Each one decides, on their own, whether to become a client of the new firm — a decision that has to rest on real information, not a hallway conversation. In practice that means a signed advisory agreement, delivery of the new firm's disclosure brochure and any related supplement, and enough plain explanation of what is changing (the firm, the fees, the custodian) that the client's yes is informed.

Keep that consent separate from the paperwork that follows it. Agreeing to become a client of the new firm is not the same as authorizing a specific account to move to a specific custodian; that authorization is its own document, and the next step depends on it existing in writing.

### Move the accounts

With that authorization in hand, the account moves through a standardized transfer process that most custodians and [broker-dealers](/glossary#broker-dealer) participate in: you submit a transfer request identifying the account and the receiving custodian, the two institutions exchange it electronically, and the account's assets move without the client selling out of positions and repurchasing them. The window for each side runs from when the transfer instruction is established or received, not from any later agreement between the two firms; see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#leave-and-transition-clients) for how long each one lasts.

Not everything moves cleanly. Some account types, proprietary investments, or unusual holdings can require extra paperwork or cannot transfer in kind, so flag those cases to the client before the request goes in rather than after it stalls. By the end of this step, you hold a firm with clients who chose it freely, signed a real advisory agreement, and whose accounts have actually landed at the new custodian — a firm doing business, not just registered to.

For illustration, the adviser reaches the step where the book stops being a number. About 80 clients sit at the old firm, and none of them transfer automatically: each one decides separately, and each yes has to be given the brochure, walked through what is changing about the firm and the fees, and asked to sign an advisory agreement and, as a separate document, an authorization to move the account.

Doing all of that alone is the constraint the step actually runs into. There is no colleague to hold a second conversation in parallel, and no transfer request can go in until its own conversation has finished, so the adviser sequences the outreach rather than announcing to everyone at once and starts with the households whose accounts look simplest to move. The runway estimate made back at the first step assumed months rather than weeks for exactly this reason, and because some of the 80 will stay behind.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Have you reviewed your employment agreement and any non-solicitation or non-compete terms before contacting a single client?
- Do you know whether your current firm participates in a recruiting protocol, and exactly what it does and does not authorize you to do before and after you resign?
- Have you gotten counsel on what information and records you can bring with you, specific to your own agreements and your state's law, rather than relying on general practice?
- Does every client moving to the new firm have a signed advisory agreement and a documented delivery of the new firm's disclosure brochure?
- Have you confirmed, in writing, that each client actually authorized their specific account transfer, separate from agreeing to become a client?

## Common mistakes

- Discussing the move, or gathering client information, before actually resigning — the timing rules that protect you under a recruiting protocol, where one applies, are specific and unforgiving.
- Treating "what can I take" as a single yes-or-no answer instead of a fact-specific question that depends on your own agreements and your state's law.
- Letting a client's general enthusiasm about following you stand in for the specific, documented consent an advisory agreement and an account-transfer authorization actually require.
- Assuming every account will transfer cleanly and finding out about an exception only after a transfer request has already stalled.
- Starting client conversations before the custody and technology work from the previous step is actually finished, leaving nowhere for a transferring account to land.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision): the resignation timing, recruiting-protocol analysis, and account-transfer authorizations in this step all sit inside the firm's compliance program from day one.
- [Risk and Insurance](/domains/risk-and-insurance): a mishandled departure is one of the more common sources of disputes and claims against a newly independent adviser, which is exactly what the firm's insurance exists to address.
- [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation): the signed advisory agreements, disclosure-delivery records, and transfer authorizations this step produces are records the firm has to keep and be able to produce later.
- [Client Education](/domains/client-education): clients can only give informed consent to the move if you explain, in plain language, what is actually changing about the firm, the fees, and the custodian.
- [Marketing and Growth](/domains/marketing-and-growth): the clients who follow you are the book the firm starts with, and its first source of referrals.

<Callout type="info">
In practice, this step rarely waits for the previous one to finish. Client conversations and account-transfer paperwork often start while the custodial relationship and operational systems from the previous step are still being tested, because the calendar of resigning and reaching out to clients does not pause for internal readiness. Treat the two steps as running in parallel, even though this guide presents them in sequence, and confirm the operational side is far enough along before collecting transfer authorizations you cannot yet execute.
</Callout>
