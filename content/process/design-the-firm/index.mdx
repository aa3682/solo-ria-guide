# Design the Firm

Design the Firm settles the shape of the practice you are building, before any legal filing or regulatory step touches it. It answers three plain questions: who the firm serves, what it offers them, and how the work gets done day to day. Pricing mechanics, the legal entity, and which regulator you register with each have their own step later in the guide; here, you are only deciding the shape those later steps will apply to.

## Why it matters

Every step that follows this one needs a specific firm to work with, not an abstract one. Set Your Advice Approach assumes you already know roughly who you are advising. Form the Business assumes you know how many people the entity needs to accommodate. The disclosure documents you write several steps from now describe, in detail, the services you actually provide — which means the description has to already exist by the time you write it.

Skipping this step, or leaving it vague, does not make the decisions go away. It just pushes them later, into steps that assume the decisions are already made, where changing your mind costs more. A firm designed on paper before you form an entity or file anything is far cheaper to redesign than one you discover doesn't fit only after clients are already on board.

## What actually happens

### Pick who you serve

Start with the client. Describe, in one or two ordinary sentences, the kind of person or household the firm is built around: their stage of life, the kind of financial questions they tend to have, and roughly how many of them you expect to serve. A firm built around "anyone with money to invest" is not a design decision — it is the absence of one, and it makes every later choice about service and marketing harder rather than easier.

### Decide what you offer

Once you know who you serve, decide what you actually do for them. Is the firm built around one clearly described service, or a small set of them? Does the work happen once and then largely stop, or does it continue for as long as the relationship lasts? You do not need pricing figures yet — that belongs to its own step — but you do need to know, in plain language, what a client is actually paying for.

### Decide how the firm is staffed

Decide whether you are building this alone or with others from day one: partners, an early hire, or outside help for the parts of the business you do not want to run yourself. Staffing shapes almost everything downstream, from how much the firm can take on to how its [supervision](/glossary#supervision) and recordkeeping have to work. A solo firm and a firm with a second adviser or support staff are, in practical terms, different businesses to design.

### Write down the design

Put the design in writing, in a paragraph you could hand to someone else and have them understand the firm in under a minute. For illustration, the adviser — introduced in the previous step, with a book of about 80 clients and roughly $300,000 in annual revenue — settles on a solo practice with no employees, one ongoing service built around financial planning and investment management together, and a single client type: working professionals within about a decade of retirement who want continuing help rather than a one-time plan. This is one shape among many an independent firm can take, not a recommended default — a specialist practice built around one narrow topic, or a small team serving a broader range of households, would each start from a different design. What matters at the end of this step is that your own version of that paragraph exists somewhere you can point back to.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Can you describe your client, in one or two sentences, without naming a product, platform, or account type?
- If someone outside the industry read your service description, would they understand what they are actually paying for?
- Are you building this alone, and if not, who else is involved from the start?
- Does your client description match the clients your current book actually contains, or the ones you wish it contained?
- If you served only the client type you just described, would there be enough of them to build a firm around?
- What would make you revisit this design later — and would you notice if that happened?

## Common mistakes

- Describing the client so broadly that the description does not actually rule anyone out. A client type that fits almost everyone does not function as a design decision.
- Deciding what you offer by copying your current firm's menu of services instead of asking what you actually want to keep doing.
- Treating staffing as an afterthought, then discovering later that supervision, recordkeeping, or a hoped-for hire depend on a firm shape you never actually settled on.
- Skipping the written version of the design and carrying it only as a general sense of direction, which tends to drift as later steps introduce their own pressures.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Service and Pricing Models](/domains/service-and-pricing-models): What you decide to offer here is the raw material that area turns into an actual pricing structure.
- [Advice and Planning Process](/domains/advice-and-planning-process): The client type and service you settle on here shape what your advice process needs to cover.

- [Client Education](/domains/client-education): Who the firm serves decides how much has to be explained, and in what words, for years afterward.
- [Marketing and Growth](/domains/marketing-and-growth): You cannot describe the firm to a stranger until you have settled here who it is for.
- [Firm Economics](/domains/firm-economics): How you are staffed and what you offer both feed directly into how the firm supports itself financially.

<Callout type="info">
At this step, you are not yet describing the firm to clients or regulators — you are describing it to yourself, clearly enough that later drafting goes faster. Document the client description, the service or services you settled on, and the staffing decision in the same place you kept your notes from the previous step, and date it. When you reach the disclosure documents several steps from now, this page is what you will be translating into formal language.
</Callout>
