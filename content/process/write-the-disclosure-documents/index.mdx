# Write the Disclosure Documents

This step turns everything settled so far into the documents a client actually reads before hiring you. Two are written in plain narrative prose: one describes the firm, the other describes the person who will give the advice. A third piece is written for your state regulator rather than for clients. All of it is drafted now and submitted later, in [File and Get Approved](/process/file-and-get-approved).

## Why it matters

These are the first documents a regulator reads and often the only ones a client ever reads closely. A state's review of your application is largely a review of what you wrote here: the reviewer compares what the documents promise against what the firm is set up to do.

They are also the firm's standing description of itself, so the writing does not end at approval. What they say has to keep matching what the firm does, which makes a change to the fee schedule or the service model a change to the documents too. That obligation recurs yearly, in [Stay Registered and Compliant](/process/stay-registered-and-compliant).

## What actually happens

### Write the firm brochure

The firm brochure tells a prospective client what your firm does, what it charges, and where your interests and theirs could pull apart. It is narrative, written in plain English and in the firm's voice rather than the language of a filing. The uniform registration form for advisers, [Form ADV](https://www.sec.gov/about/forms/formadv-instructions.pdf), sets the topics it must cover and their order; the brochure is its Part 2A.

You do not choose the topics, but you choose everything said under them. They reach the firm's advisory business and fees, the clients it serves, how it analyzes and recommends, any disciplinary history, outside business activities, and whether the firm holds client assets or exercises discretion. Write each from your own decisions in the earlier steps.

### Write the supplement for the person giving the advice

The brochure describes the firm; a second and much shorter document describes the individual who will actually advise the client. It covers that person's education and business background, any disciplinary history, other business activities, outside compensation, and who supervises the advice. This is Part 2B of the same form, and one is prepared for each person who advises.

In a solo firm the supplement is about you, and supervision is the question that takes thought. The only supervised person is also the owner, so the honest answer describes how you review your own work rather than naming someone above you. Say what that arrangement is, because the policies you write next have to match it.

### Complete the state-only portion of the filing

A state-registered adviser also completes a part of the registration form that only state-registered advisers fill in, and no client receives it. It answers your state's questions about the firm on subjects such as bonding, custody, and financial condition, and it is Part 1B of the form. Draft it now, because its answers have to agree with the brochure.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: you skip the state-only portion described above, since only a state-registered adviser completes it. You also prepare a short client relationship summary, filed as Form CRS — a standardized document for retail clients that sits alongside the firm brochure rather than replacing it, and that a state-registered adviser does not prepare at all. Its page limit and its delivery deadlines are set by SEC rule and belong on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures).
</Callout>

### Decide who receives which document, and when

Every prospective client receives the firm brochure and the supplement for whoever will advise them, around the point the advisory agreement is signed. State rules set how far ahead of signing that has to happen, and some allow delivery at signing but give the client a window to terminate without penalty. Both figures are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures).

After that, delivery becomes a yearly obligation to existing clients: either the current brochure, or a summary of what changed materially since the last one with an offer to provide the full document. That deadline is set by rule and is on the same page. A material change between cycles, particularly anything disciplinary, goes out when it happens.

### Update on the annual cycle and in between

Once registered, the firm files an annual updating amendment after its fiscal year ends, confirming or correcting every answer on the form. The deadline runs from that year end and is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). If anything material changed, the brochure carries a summary of those changes, at its front or as a separate document sent with it.

Some answers cannot wait for that cycle. The form's instructions identify the ones that must be amended promptly when they stop being accurate, and disciplinary and custody answers are most often in that group. The habit that keeps this manageable: when the firm changes something the brochure describes, change the brochure in the same sitting.

### Keep a record of what you delivered, to whom, and when

You have to be able to show that a client received a particular version of a document on a particular date. That means keeping a dated copy of every version as delivered, not just the current one, and a log of the deliveries. How long they must be kept is set by rule and is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures). For a solo firm a spreadsheet is enough: client, document, version date, delivery date.

For illustration, the adviser, solo and serving working professionals within about a decade of retirement, writes the brochure from the firm's own decisions rather than from a template. The fee schedule is the one the firm will actually charge, the account of how advice is developed is the approach settled earlier, and the conflicts section names only the ones a firm with one owner and no outside compensation really has. The supplement describes the adviser's background and states plainly that the only supervised person is the owner, and how that review works. The adviser builds the delivery log before the first client exists, so the first delivery is recorded rather than reconstructed later.

You leave this step holding a drafted firm brochure, a drafted supplement for yourself, the state-only portion answered consistently with both, a written note of the delivery and update deadlines your state sets, and a place to record deliveries. None of it is filed yet: it goes into the folder from earlier steps and is submitted in [File and Get Approved](/process/file-and-get-approved).

## Questions to ask yourself

- Does every service, fee, and conflict the brochure describes match what your firm will actually do on its first day?
- If a prospective client read the brochure and nothing else, would they understand what they are buying and what it costs?
- Who supervises the advice in a firm where you are the only person, and can you describe that arrangement honestly?
- A year from now, how will you know which version a client received?

## Common mistakes

- Starting from a template found elsewhere and leaving in services the firm does not offer, or conflicts it does not have.
- Describing a fee schedule that differs, even slightly, from the one in the client agreement.
- Disclosing conflicts in the abstract, as things that might arise, rather than naming the ones that exist.
- Treating delivery as a one-time act, and keeping no record of which client received which version.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Service and Pricing Models](/domains/service-and-pricing-models): The brochure's fee and service sections are that area's decisions, written down for a client and a regulator to read.
- [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision): Keeping the documents accurate, delivering them on time, and updating them is an ongoing obligation your compliance program owns.
- [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation): Every version and every delivery is a record an examiner may ask to see.

<Callout type="info">
Write the brochure and the client agreement side by side, because an examiner reads them together and a gap between the fees, services, or termination terms is the first thing found. Keep every version dated and never overwrite the previous one: the question is always what a client received at the time, not what the document says now. [NASAA](https://www.nasaa.org/industry-resources/investment-advisers/) collects the state regulators' adviser resources in one place.
</Callout>
