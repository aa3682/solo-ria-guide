# Write the Policies

The last step wrote what a client and a regulator read about the firm. This one writes what the firm reads about itself: the internal documents that say how it will actually run, catch its own mistakes, and stay inside the rules once clients exist. Several of these documents are required by rule to exist in writing before the firm can be approved, and all of them have to describe a real firm rather than a downloaded template.

## Why it matters

A registered adviser must adopt and implement written policies and procedures reasonably designed to prevent violations of the rules that govern it. That requirement is the reason this step exists, and an examiner treats the manual the same way a state treats the brochure: as a description to test against what the firm actually does, not a formality to file and forget.

The stakes are different for a solo firm than for a larger one. There is no separate compliance department to lean on, and no one else to notice if a policy quietly stops matching practice. What you write here becomes the standard you hold yourself to, and the habit of keeping it current carries into [Stay Registered and Compliant](/process/stay-registered-and-compliant).

## What actually happens

### Write the compliance manual

The compliance manual is the written description of how your firm supervises itself: who reviews client accounts and how often, how conflicts get identified and handled, how marketing materials are checked before they go out, and how the firm protects client data. In a solo firm, the honest answer to "who administers this program" is the owner, and the manual should say so plainly rather than describing a review structure the firm doesn't have.

Write each section from decisions you have already made in earlier steps — your service model, your fee structure, your advice approach — rather than from a generic list of topics. A manual that describes a firm that doesn't exist is worse than no manual at all, because it gives an examiner a document to compare against reality and find wrong.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: the rule that requires this program also requires you to designate a [chief compliance officer](/glossary#chief-compliance-officer) by name and title. A [state-registered adviser](/glossary#state-registered-adviser)'s obligation to name someone comes from state rule instead, and some states do not require the title at all for a firm with one owner.
</Callout>

### Adopt a code of ethics

A [code of ethics](/glossary#code-of-ethics) sets the standard of conduct you hold yourself to as a fiduciary, and covers the part that is easy to overlook: your own personal securities transactions. It should say what you must do before trading in a security you also recommend to clients, how you report your own holdings and trades, and how you handle gifts and outside business activity that could color your advice.

Write rules you can actually follow as a firm of one. A pre-clearance step that requires checking with someone else only works if that someone else exists; in a solo firm the workable version is a personal log you keep and review against client trading yourself, on a schedule you write down and keep to.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: [Advisers Act Rule 204A-1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-II/part-275/section-275.204A-1) requires a code of ethics in a specific form. A state-registered adviser's code comes from state rule, most often modeled closely on the same requirement, but the details can differ, so confirm your state's version rather than assuming it matches.
</Callout>

### Write the privacy policy

Your privacy policy describes what personal information you collect from clients, how you use and share it, and how you protect it, and it has to be delivered to clients rather than just kept on file. Write it to match what your firm actually does with client data — the systems it uses, whether it shares information with any outside party for a business purpose, and how it secures records containing personal information.

Keep this document short and specific. A privacy policy copied from elsewhere often promises safeguards the firm hasn't actually built, or describes sharing practices that don't match the firm's real vendors and systems.

### Write the business continuity and succession plan

This plan answers a question a larger firm can absorb without much thought and a solo firm cannot: what happens to client accounts if you are unreachable, incapacitated, or unable to work for an extended stretch. It should cover how the firm keeps running through a shorter disruption — a lost office, a failed system, a regional outage — and, separately, who is authorized to step in and act for clients if the owner cannot.

For a firm of one, the succession piece is the part most often skipped and the one regulators and clients most need answered. Name a specific person, describe what authority they would have and how they would get it, and put the arrangement in writing rather than leaving it as an intention.

### Build the recordkeeping scheme

The last piece is the plan for keeping every record the firm is required to keep: client agreements, correspondence, advertising, financial records, and the compliance documents you just wrote, among others. Decide the format each record lives in, where it is stored, who can access it, and how it is backed up. How long each category must be retained, and how quickly it must be produced if a regulator asks, are set by rule and belong on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#write-the-policies), not here.

By the end of this step you hold a finished, adviser-specific policy set: a compliance manual, a code of ethics, a privacy policy, a [business continuity and succession plan](/glossary#business-continuity-and-succession-plan), and a recordkeeping scheme that says where everything lives. None of it has been tested against real operation yet — that starts once the firm is approved and running, in later steps — but it is complete enough to file.

For illustration, the adviser, solo with no employees, writes each document around the one fact that makes it honest: the owner runs the program and is the only supervised person, which is what the brochure supplement already tells clients. The code of ethics is a personal trading log reviewed on a written schedule, not a pre-clearance step routed to someone who does not exist. Succession is the hardest piece — with no colleague to inherit the accounts, the adviser arranges for another solo adviser in the same state to step in, and puts that authority in writing rather than leaving it an understanding. The recordkeeping scheme then says where each of these documents lives.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Does the compliance manual describe procedures your firm will actually follow, or procedures copied from somewhere else?
- If you are the only person in the firm, who reviews your own work, and how often?
- If you were unreachable for weeks, could a named person find client accounts and act on them?
- Are the personal trading rules in your code of ethics ones you can realistically keep to yourself?
- Do you know, specifically, where each category of record will live and how you would retrieve it?

## Common mistakes

- Adopting a manual or code of ethics found online without rewriting it to match the firm's actual services, systems, and size.
- Writing personal trading rules that assume someone else will do the checking, in a firm where no one else exists.
- Treating the business continuity plan as a data-backup checklist and leaving out who is authorized to act for clients if the owner cannot.
- Designing a [recordkeeping scheme](/glossary#recordkeeping) around "keep everything" without deciding where any specific record actually lives.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Technology and Client Experience](/domains/technology-and-client-experience): the privacy policy and the security and continuity procedures written here set the rules the firm's systems have to meet.
- [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision): this step produces the core documents that area covers running day to day, for the rest of the firm's life.
- [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation): the recordkeeping scheme built here is that area's subject, applied for the first time.
- [Risk and Insurance](/domains/risk-and-insurance): the business continuity and succession plan is one of the ways a firm manages the risk of its own disruption.

<Callout type="info">
Date and version every document in this set the way you will the brochure, and keep superseded versions rather than overwriting them — an examiner may ask what your policy said on a specific date in the past, not just what it says now. Many states base these requirements on [NASAA's model rule for written policies and procedures](https://www.nasaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/NASAA-IA-PandP-Model-Rule-and-Sample-Compliance-Grid.pdf), which covers the whole set — compliance and supervision, code of ethics, business continuity and succession, and the rest — in one rule and calls for reviewing it on a set schedule. The required frequency is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#compliance-and-supervision).
</Callout>
