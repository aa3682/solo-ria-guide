# Set Your Advice Approach

Having settled who the firm serves and what it offers, this step settles how the firm actually advises: what your investment philosophy is, how you decide what to recommend, and what you will and will not do for clients. It does not produce disclosure language or written policies — those come later — but it produces the substance those later steps will turn into documents.

## Why it matters

The disclosure documents you write several steps from now describe your advisory business practices in detail: your methods of analysis, your sources of information, the discretion you exercise, and the material risks of your approach. You cannot describe any of that honestly until you have actually decided it. Writing a disclosure document around an approach you never settled tends to produce vague, boilerplate language that describes no firm in particular.

The written policies you build after that assume an approach already exists to write procedures around. A firm that has not decided how it decides cannot write a coherent process for reviewing recommendations, documenting rationale, or supervising the advice it gives, because there is nothing specific yet to supervise.

Skipping this step does not remove the decision — it just defers it to a moment when changing your mind costs more, because a filed disclosure document and a client-facing process are both harder to revise than a page of notes. Settling the approach now, while it only has to satisfy you, is cheaper than settling it later while it also has to satisfy a regulator and a client relationship already underway.

## What actually happens

### Decide your investment philosophy

Start with how you actually think about markets and risk, stated plainly enough that it would guide a real recommendation rather than sit as a slogan. Do you build portfolios around broad diversification held through market cycles, or do you shift positioning in response to changing conditions? How do you think about the tradeoff between risk and expected return, and how does that thinking change for a client near retirement compared to one decades away? None of this requires naming a specific product, fund family, or model provider — it requires a position you could explain to a client in a few plain sentences and then actually follow.

### Decide how much discretion you take

Separate from philosophy is a narrower, more operational question: once you have a recommendation, who acts on it? Discretionary authority means you can place trades or rebalance a client's account without asking each time, within limits you have agreed to in advance. Non-discretionary means the client approves each action before it happens. This choice shapes your daily workload, how quickly the firm can respond to a client's changing situation, and what your compliance program later has to supervise — it is not a detail to leave until the paperwork stage.

### Decide the boundaries of what you do and don't do

Decide, in plain terms, what falls inside your advice and what you will consistently send elsewhere. Typical boundaries include tax return preparation, legal document drafting, and insurance product sales — matters an advice conversation can touch without the firm doing the work itself, unless you decide otherwise. Where exactly you draw that line is your decision, but the line has to exist before a client asks a question that sits near it. An approach with no stated boundary tends to have its boundary set, inconsistently, by whichever question comes up first.

### Write down the approach

Put the approach in writing, specific enough that someone else could read it and understand how you would actually treat a real client's situation. For illustration, the adviser — introduced in earlier steps, solo, no employees, one ongoing service combining planning and investment management, serving working professionals within about a decade of retirement — settles on an approach built around broadly diversified, low-cost portfolios matched to each client's time horizon and risk tolerance. Adjustments happen at agreed check-ins rather than in reaction to daily market moves, and the adviser holds discretionary authority to rebalance and make changes within limits set out in advance, so clients are not asked to approve routine trades.

The scope stays inside investments and the planning built around them: the adviser reviews a client's tax and estate documents for coordination but refers the client to their own tax preparer and attorney for anything that requires one. This is one approach among several a firm like this could take — a similarly built firm serving the same clients could just as reasonably choose non-discretionary management or a more active philosophy instead. What matters at the end of this step is that your own version of that paragraph exists, not that it matches anyone else's.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Could you explain your investment philosophy to a client in a few plain sentences, without naming a specific product, fund, or manager?
- Do you want discretionary authority to act on a client's account, or do you want to ask before every change — and have you weighed what each choice costs you in time and responsiveness?
- Are there kinds of advice, like tax preparation or legal drafting, that you will consistently decline to give yourself, and do you know where you will send a client who needs one?
- If two clients came to you with similar goals but different risk tolerances, would your stated approach actually produce different recommendations for each of them?
- Does this approach match the way you actually advised clients at your current firm, or is it a change you are making deliberately?
- What would make you conclude, later, that this approach doesn't fit the clients you actually have?

## Common mistakes

- Describing your philosophy in marketing language rather than in terms specific enough to guide an actual recommendation.
- Deciding discretionary authority as an afterthought instead of a deliberate choice with real operational and supervisory consequences.
- Leaving the boundaries of what you do and don't do undefined, so each client conversation re-litigates what is included.
- Carrying over your current firm's approach wholesale without asking whether it actually fits the firm you designed in the previous step.
