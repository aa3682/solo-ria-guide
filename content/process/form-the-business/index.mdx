# Form the Business

Form the Business turns the firm you have designed on paper into a business that exists in law. It covers the entity form, the state of formation, the name, the registered agent, the federal tax identification number, business banking, and keeping the entity's records apart from your own. It does not register anything with a securities regulator — that comes later — but it produces the legal entity those later steps register.

## Why it matters

Registration as an investment adviser is done by a specific legal person, and the steps that follow assume that person already exists. [Choose Your Regulator](/process/choose-your-regulator) asks where the firm registers, which presumes there is a firm. The disclosure documents describe the entity by its legal name, form, and owners, so each of those has to be settled before it can be written down.

This is also the first step whose subject is legal and tax rather than design. Earlier steps asked what kind of firm you want; this one asks what legal structure carries it, and the right answer depends on your own tax situation, your state, and your plans. Which option fits you is a question for your own attorney or tax professional, not something a guide can settle.

## What actually happens

### Choose an entity form

Every state offers several ways to organize a business, and the details differ by state, so what follows is general. A sole proprietorship is you doing business with no separate entity at all. A limited liability company, corporation, or partnership is a separate legal person that owns the firm's contracts and accounts and generally stands between the firm's obligations and your personal assets.

The entity form and its tax treatment are separate decisions. Some forms can be taxed in more than one way, and choosing among those ways is an election you make with the IRS, with consequences for how you pay yourself and what you owe. Which combination suits your firm depends on your income, your state, and your plans, and it is a question for your own tax professional.

One more constraint is specific to advisers. The state securities regulator you will register with may have rules that bear on entity choice — on whether an adviser can register as an individual rather than through an entity, or on how the entity's name must appear on the registration. Those rules vary by state and are not worked through here. Read your state's requirements before you file, so the entity you create is one the regulator will accept.

### Choose where to form it and what to call it

Where you form the entity is separate from where you will work. Forming outside your home state usually means registering there anyway as an out-of-state business, which adds a second set of filings and fees. Whether any reason exists for your firm to form elsewhere is a question for your attorney.

The entity's name has to be available in the state of formation, which generally means it is distinguishable from names already on file. Some states restrict certain words in entity names or require a suffix that identifies the entity form. Because the name will appear on your registration and disclosure documents, check it against your state's business name records and your regulator's naming rules together.

### Appoint a registered agent and file

A registered agent is a person or business with a physical address in the state of formation who accepts legal and official mail for the entity. States require one for entities like these, though who may serve and what address qualifies vary. You can usually act as your own agent if you have a qualifying address in the state; either way, the agent's name and address become part of the public record.

With the form, state, name, and agent settled, you file the formation document with the state's business filing office and pay its filing fee. The document's name, the office, the fee, and any yearly franchise tax or annual report that follows all differ by state; the amounts live on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures), not here. Once the state accepts the filing, the entity exists.

### Get a tax identification number and open business banking

An employer identification number, or EIN, is the federal tax identification number the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number) assigns to a business. You obtain it directly from the IRS, which does not charge for it, and you will need it to open a business bank account, file entity tax returns, and complete later registration forms. Apply once the state has accepted the formation filing.

With the formation document and the EIN in hand, open a bank account in the entity's name. The bank will want both, along with whatever governing document the entity form calls for, such as an operating agreement or bylaws. From this point the firm's money moves through this account and nothing else: client fees are paid to it, expenses are paid from it, and you pay yourself out of it.

### Write down what you now hold

Keep the filed formation document, the governing document, the EIN confirmation, the registered agent details, and the bank records together, apart from your personal papers. That separation is what makes the entity's existence real if it is ever tested, and what later recordkeeping rules will expect to find.

For illustration, the adviser — introduced in earlier steps, solo, no employees, serving working professionals within about a decade of retirement — faces the decision in its simplest shape. There is one owner, so the questions are about liability, tax treatment, and what the home state's securities regulator will accept, rather than about partners or shares. The adviser reads the home state's registration requirements first, then takes the tax question to a tax professional and the formation question to an attorney, and forms the entity in the home state under a name the state and the regulator both accept. At the end of the step the adviser holds a filed formation document, a governing document, an EIN, a registered agent on record, a business bank account, and a folder that keeps all of it apart from personal affairs.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Do your state's adviser registration requirements constrain the entity form or name you are considering?
- Have you taken the tax treatment question to someone qualified to answer it for your situation?
- Is there a reason for your firm to form anywhere other than the state where you live and work?
- Is the name you want available, and does it satisfy both the state's naming rules and your regulator's?
- Will you serve as your own registered agent, and is your address one you are comfortable seeing in the public record?

## Common mistakes

- Choosing an entity form because other advisers seem to use it, rather than because your own attorney or tax professional looked at your situation.
- Treating the tax election as automatic, and finding out at tax time that it is taxed in a way you never chose.
- Filing formation documents before checking whether the regulator's rules accept that entity form or that name.
- Paying firm expenses from a personal account, or personal expenses from the firm's.
- Assuming that a separate entity makes professional liability coverage unnecessary. The entity's shield and the firm's insurance address different risks.
