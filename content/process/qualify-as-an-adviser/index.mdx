# Qualify as an Adviser

Every step so far has been about the firm; this one is about you. A firm registers as an investment adviser, but the person who actually gives the advice registers separately, as an [investment adviser representative](/glossary#investment-adviser-representative), with requirements of its own. In a solo firm you are both the firm's principal and its only representative, so this step is where you establish that you, personally, qualify.

## Why it matters

The firm's registration, filed in a later step, cannot be approved without a qualified representative attached to it. A state reviewing the firm's application reads the individual filing alongside it, and a missing exam, an incomplete disclosure, or a fingerprint check still pending holds up the whole thing.

The individual requirements are also the part most likely to surprise someone coming from a [broker-dealer](/glossary#broker-dealer), where a registration department handled all of this. Now the firm is you, and none of it ends at approval: the filing has to stay accurate for as long as you are registered, and most states now require yearly continuing education, which becomes part of the routine in [Stay Registered and Compliant](/process/stay-registered-and-compliant).

## What actually happens

### Establish that you meet the exam requirement

Before registering, a representative has to demonstrate competence, usually by passing a [qualifying exam](/glossary#qualifying-examination). The exam is written under the sponsorship of [NASAA](https://www.nasaa.org/exams/), the association of state securities regulators, and administered by [FINRA](/glossary#finra). Which exam, or combination of exams, a state accepts is set by state rule, and the identifiers live on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser).

Certain [professional designations](/glossary#professional-designation) can substitute for the exam in states that permit it. Which designations a state accepts, if any, is a state choice, and the list is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser) as well.

### Check whether a prior exam still counts

As a general matter, a passed exam stays valid for as long as you remain registered in a role that relies on it, and lapses after a period without any such registration. That period is set by state rule, and some states will consider waiving a retake on a showing of experience; both belong on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser).

What matters is the gap in your registration history, not how long ago you sat for the exam. Your record in [CRD](/glossary#crd) shows the dates, and your home state's regulator can tell you how it reads them.

### Prepare the individual filing

The firm and the person are two separate filings. The firm registers on its own form, the subject of [File and Get Approved](/process/file-and-get-approved). You register on a separate [uniform form for individuals](https://www.finra.org/registration-exams-ce/broker-dealers/registration-forms/form-u4), filed through CRD, which FINRA operates for both broker-dealer and adviser registrations. Both usually go in together, but approval of one is not approval of the other.

If you have worked at a broker-dealer, the new filing attaches to the record you already have there. It asks for your employment history, other business activities, and where you will work. The state charges a fee for each representative, which appears on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser).

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: your firm registers with the [SEC](/glossary#securities-and-exchange-commission), but you do not. Under [section 203A of the Investment Advisers Act](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section80b-3a&num=0&edition=prelim), a state may still register a representative who has a place of business there, so your individual filing goes to your home state, which still sets the exam, fingerprint, and continuing-education requirements that apply to you.
</Callout>

The disclosure section deserves the most care. It asks yes-or-no questions about your history: criminal charges and convictions, regulatory actions, civil suits involving investments, customer complaints, terminations from prior firms, and financial events such as bankruptcies, unpaid judgments, and liens. A yes answer does not by itself bar registration, because the regulator weighs what happened and when. An inaccurate or incomplete answer is different, since the filing is signed as true and a false statement is grounds for denial or discipline on its own.

Answer against the record, not from memory: pull your current record, read every prior answer, and reconcile anything that has changed.

### Handle the state requirements beyond the exam

Beyond the exam, your state asks for something you complete once before registering, and something that repeats every year you stay registered.

Many states require fingerprints from a representative of a state-registered firm so they can run a criminal background check. How the fingerprints are taken and submitted differs by state, and whether your state requires them, and what it charges, is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser). A background check is also where an incomplete disclosure surfaces.

The recurring piece is continuing education. Most states now require a representative to complete it every year, following a [model rule NASAA adopted](https://www.nasaa.org/industry-resources/investment-advisers/investment-adviser-representative-continuing-education/) that each state puts into effect on its own schedule. The model has two parts: one on ethics and professional responsibility, and one on products, practices, and the rules that govern them. The yearly credit counts, and whether your state has adopted the rule, are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser).

The obligation begins once you are registered. What you decide here is how you will track it, because a representative who misses a year is generally blocked from renewing until it is made up, and in a solo firm no one else is watching the calendar.

### Confirm with your home state and write it down

For illustration, the adviser, solo and coming from a broker-dealer, works through three questions. The first is whether the exam already on the adviser's record still counts. The adviser has been continuously registered with no gap, but confirms that reading with the home state rather than assuming it, since the state's rule is what governs.

The second is what the individual filing asks about the adviser's history. The adviser pulls the current record, reads each disclosure answer as the broker-dealer filed it, and confirms that nothing has changed. The third is what the home state requires beyond the exam: the adviser reads the state's requirements page, finds that it requires fingerprints and a representative fee and has adopted the continuing-education rule, and confirms all three with the state's registration staff in a single call.

You leave this step holding evidence that you meet the exam requirement; a draft individual filing with every disclosure answer checked against the record; fingerprints taken or scheduled as your state requires; and a written note of the state's fee, its continuing-education rule, and what its staff confirmed. It goes into the folder from earlier steps.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Does your registration history have a gap that could affect whether a prior exam still counts, and have you asked the state rather than assumed?
- If you are relying on a designation instead of the exam, does your home state accept it?
- Have you read your current disclosure answers as they were actually filed, rather than answering from memory?
- Is there anything in your history, such as a complaint, a termination, or a financial event, that you would rather not disclose?
- Who will track your continuing-education deadline once the firm is running?

## Common mistakes

- Assuming an exam passed years ago still counts, without checking your registration history against your state's rule.
- Treating a designation as a waiver everywhere, when acceptance is decided state by state and some states accept none.
- Answering the disclosure questions from memory and contradicting your own prior filing.
- Leaving a financial event off the filing because it feels unrelated to advising, when the form asks about it directly.

## Which practice areas apply

- [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision): Keeping the individual filing accurate and completing continuing education are yearly obligations your compliance program owns, even when it supervises only you.
- [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation): Exam records, continuing-education completion, and the filing as submitted are records an examiner may ask to see.
- [Firm Economics](/domains/firm-economics): Exam, fingerprint, and representative fees belong in the runway estimate, and some recur every year.

<Callout type="info">
The disclosure section is public, and clients can read it on the search sites regulators run, so anything that needs explaining is better disclosed with context than found later. Keep the state's confirmation, the exam evidence, and a copy of the filing as submitted in the folder from earlier steps. [NASAA](https://www.nasaa.org/industry-resources/investment-advisers/) collects the state regulators' adviser resources in one place.
</Callout>
