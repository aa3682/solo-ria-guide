# Building Your Running Calendar

Every page in this guide before this one produces something that finishes. This one produces an instrument you reopen on a schedule for as long as the firm operates, and [Stay Registered and Compliant](/process/stay-registered-and-compliant) is where it comes from.

## What this measures

Not a quantity. This checklist assembles the calendar a registered firm runs on: what it owes, when each thing falls due, and what each one leaves behind as evidence that it happened.

The reason a calendar does this work rather than a memory is that nothing here announces itself. A filing deadline arrives whether or not you thought about it, and the obligations that go wrong in a solo firm are rarely the ones anyone found difficult — they are the ones nobody was watching for.

## Before you start

Have these to hand, since most dates on the calendar are derived from them rather than chosen:

- Your registration's effective date and the firm's fiscal year end.
- Your delivery log from [Write the Disclosure Documents](/process/write-the-disclosure-documents), showing who has received which version of what.
- The review schedule you wrote into your own policy set in [Write the Policies](/process/write-the-policies).
- The state or states where the firm is registered or makes filings, since several items below are set state by state.

## What recurs on the annual cycle

These fall due once a year, every year. Each is timed from a date the firm does not pick, which is why the middle column matters more than the deadline itself.

| Recurring item | Timed from | Evidence it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Annual updating amendment](/glossary#annual-updating-amendment) | Fiscal year end | The filed amendment, and a note of what changed |
| Registration renewal | The regulator's renewal notice | Payment confirmation and renewal status |
| Brochure or summary-of-changes delivery to existing clients | Fiscal year end | A dated log entry per client |
| Continuing education for the representative | The calendar year | Completion records |
| [Compliance program review](/glossary#compliance-program-review) | The schedule your own policies set | A dated record of what was checked, found, and changed |

Every deadline, window, credit count and fee behind that table is set by rule or by state and lives on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures) — the amendment deadline and the yearly delivery deadline under [Write the Disclosure Documents](/tools/this-years-figures#write-the-disclosure-documents), the [renewal window](/glossary#renewal-window) and its fee under [Stay Registered and Compliant](/tools/this-years-figures#stay-registered-and-compliant), the education credits under [Qualify as an Adviser](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser), and the review frequency under [Compliance and Supervision](/tools/this-years-figures#compliance-and-supervision). Confirm each one the year you use it rather than copying last year's date forward.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: renewal is not one home-state payment. You renew with the SEC and separately keep current a [notice filing](/glossary#notice-filing), and its fee, in every state where you do enough business to require one — so this row is a list that grows as clients spread, with the fee and the triggering level of business on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#file-and-get-approved). You also deliver a client relationship summary alongside the brochure, which a state-registered adviser never prepares.
</Callout>

## What recurs on its own trigger

These have no date. Something happens, and a clock starts — which makes them the harder half of the calendar, because the calendar cannot remind you of a date it does not have.

| Trigger | What it starts | Shape of the deadline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An answer on the registration form stops being accurate | An [other-than-annual amendment](/glossary#other-than-annual-amendment) | Promptly, not at the next cycle |
| Something material in the brochure changes between cycles | Delivery to affected clients | When it happens |
| Net worth falls below a required minimum | Notice to the regulator, then a financial report | Short, and set state by state |
| A regulator asks to see a record | Production of it | Set by rule |
| The firm starts generating a kind of record it did not before | An update to the recordkeeping scheme | None, which is the risk |

The list of form answers that require a prompt amendment is the one worth keeping visibly on the calendar even though none of them has a date: reread it on a fixed rhythm and ask what has changed, rather than waiting to notice. The reporting deadline for a net worth shortfall is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#risk-and-insurance), and the production deadline once a regulator asks is under [Write the Policies](/tools/this-years-figures#write-the-policies), alongside how long each category of record has to be kept.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: the client relationship summary adds a trigger a state-registered adviser does not have. Whenever anything in it becomes materially inaccurate it has to be amended and redelivered to existing clients, on its own deadline rather than the brochure's — see [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#write-the-disclosure-documents).
</Callout>

## The result

One dated calendar holding both lists: the annual items with their derived dates, and the triggered items with the conditions that start them. Against each, the evidence it produces and where that evidence gets filed. Count the entries and write the total at the top with the date you built it.

## What the number does and does not tell you

The count tells you how much the firm is tracking, and comparing it year to year tells you whether the calendar is keeping up with a practice that has grown.

It does not tell you the firm is compliant. A complete calendar and a firm meeting its obligations are different things, and the gap between them is filled by actually doing the work. Nor does the count prove the calendar is complete: an obligation you never identified does not appear as a missing entry, it simply is not there. And because the triggered items carry no dates, any count weighted by dates understates exactly the half that goes wrong most often.

## What to do with it

- Put the calendar where you already look every week. A compliance calendar kept somewhere separate is a second system to remember, which defeats it.
- Date the calendar itself and keep the superseded version, the same way you version the policy set it came from.
- Make reviewing the calendar an entry on the calendar. It is the only item that catches the others going stale.
- Reopen it whenever the firm changes — a new service, a new state, a first employee, a change in how fees are billed. Each of those can add a row, and none of them arrives labelled as a compliance event.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
The failure mode in a firm of one is not missing a deadline you knew about. It is the slow divergence between what the calendar says the firm does and what the firm has quietly started doing — billing differently, advising on something new, keeping records somewhere else. When you review the calendar, read it against the firm as it actually operates now rather than against the firm it described when you wrote it, and treat any row you cannot match to current practice as the finding rather than the paperwork.
</Callout>
