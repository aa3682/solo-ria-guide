# Calculating Your Regulatory Assets Under Management

This worksheet walks one calculation: the assets figure that decides whether your home state or the SEC registers your firm. [Choose Your Regulator](/process/choose-your-regulator) explains what turns on that figure; this page is where you work it out.

## What this measures

[Regulatory assets under management](/glossary#regulatory-assets-under-management) is a defined figure, not the number you would use to describe your book to a client. It counts the securities portfolios your firm continuously and regularly supervises or manages, valued gross, calculated the way the [Form ADV General Instructions](https://www.sec.gov/about/forms/formadv-instructions.pdf) define it.

The definition surprises people in both directions. The figure runs larger than expected because nothing is netted out of it, and smaller than expected because an account you advise on casually does not count at all.

## Before you start

Have these in front of you:

- Every account you expect to advise, including ones you would not describe as managed.
- A statement value for each.
- One as-of date that all of those values share.
- Your written note from [Design the Firm](/process/design-the-firm) on what the firm actually does for a client, which is what decides whether an account counts.

A firm that does not exist yet projects this figure rather than measuring it. You are counting the accounts you reasonably expect to be advising once clients have moved, not the ones you advise today at the firm you are leaving.

## Which accounts count

An account has to pass two tests.

**Is it a securities portfolio?** An account qualifies only if securities make up the bulk of its value, and cash and cash equivalents may be treated as securities for that test. The exact share is set in the Form ADV General Instructions; read the line there rather than eyeballing it.

**Do you supervise or manage it continuously and regularly?** This turns on what you actually do for the account over time, not on what you are paid or on what the client calls the relationship.

| Arrangement | Counts | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Discretionary authority](/glossary#discretionary-authority) plus ongoing management | Yes | You decide and you act, on a continuing basis |
| No discretion, but you recommend continuously and arrange the trades the client accepts | Yes | The ongoing arranging responsibility is what carries it |
| A one-time plan with no continuing role afterwards | No | Nothing continuous or regular follows |
| An account you look at when asked, and never arrange trades for | No | Commentary is not supervision |
| A family or employee account you manage for no fee | Yes | Compensation is not part of the test |

Count accounts, not households. A couple with three accounts between them contributes three entries, each counted once; a joint account is one entry, not one per owner. Where another firm supervises an account and you only see it, it belongs to that firm's figure, not yours.

## What each account is worth

Three rules do most of the work:

- **Gross, not net.** Do not subtract borrowing against the account, and do not subtract the fees you expect to charge it.
- **One date for everything.** Use a single as-of date across all accounts so the total is internally consistent. The instructions set how close that date has to be to your filing; read them before you file.
- **The whole account.** Count its full value, not the portion you think of as the part you actively work on.

A worked example, with round hypothetical numbers:

| Account | Value | In or out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Managed account, discretionary, ongoing | $1,400,000 | In |
| Second account, same household, same terms | $600,000 | In |
| Workplace retirement account you comment on twice a year | $250,000 | Out |
| One-time plan delivered last spring | $300,000 | Out |
| Borrowing against the first account | $100,000 | Not subtracted |

The total is $2,000,000 across two accounts. The $550,000 that fell out is not a rounding error — it is the definition working.

## The result

You end with two things: a total, and the number of accounts behind it. Write both down with the as-of date and with the reasoning that put each account in or out, because the form asks for the count as well as the total, and because next year you will want to know what you decided this year.

Compare the total against the lines — the ceiling below which you register with your home state, the threshold at or above which the SEC registers you, the band between them, and the buffer that keeps ordinary drift from forcing a switch. All four are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#decide-if-independence-fits).

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: the buffer is not symmetrical, so the figure that would require you to register with the SEC and the lower figure at which an already-registered firm may withdraw are two different numbers, both on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#decide-if-independence-fits). Read the one that matches the direction you are moving.
</Callout>

## What the number does and does not tell you

It tells you which regulator the rules point to, and it is the number you will report on the form itself.

It does not tell you what the firm will earn. Your fee base and this figure are different sets of accounts more often than not, so do not use one to forecast the other. It also does not settle the regulator on its own: an exception can send a firm to the SEC whatever its assets, and the one most likely to reach a solo practice is the [multi-state exception](/glossary#multi-state-exception), whose trigger is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#choose-your-regulator). Run the total first, then read the exceptions.

## What to do with it

- Keep the worksheet, not only the total, and date it. An examiner can ask how you arrived at the figure.
- Carry the total into the written determination that [Choose Your Regulator](/process/choose-your-regulator) produces, alongside the exceptions you ruled out.
- Redo it every year with the [annual updating amendment](/glossary#annual-updating-amendment), whose deadline is on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#write-the-disclosure-documents). The answer can change without you doing anything.
- Rerun it out of cycle when a large relationship arrives or leaves, so a move across a line is something you noticed rather than something you discover at the next filing.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
The figure moves with the market, which means a firm sitting close to a line can cross it in a quarter without gaining or losing a client. If your total lands anywhere near one, keep the spreadsheet live rather than rebuilding it each year from statements, and note which accounts are large enough that losing one would move the answer. That note is worth more at the next annual update than the total itself.
</Callout>
