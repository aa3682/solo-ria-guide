# Reaching Your Written Decision

This is the sheet you fill in before deciding whether to go independent at all. [Decide If Independence Fits](/process/decide-if-independence-fits) sets out what the decision involves; this page is where you put numbers against it and find out how much they actually tell you.

## What this measures

How long your household and a new firm would have to be funded before the firm pays for itself, across a range of outcomes rather than at one guess.

Step 1 splits the decision in two: a numbers question, and a preference question about whether you want to run a business at all. This sheet does the numbers half. It cannot do the other half, and a page that produced a score for it would be pretending — so the output here is one input to your decision, not the decision.

## Before you start

- Last year's revenue from your own book, broken down per client rather than as a total.
- Which of that revenue recurs without you doing anything new, and which depends on activity you would have to repeat.
- Your household's monthly spending, separately from any business figure.
- The cost categories a new firm carries. None of the amounts belong here — entity formation and the yearly state fee are on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#form-the-business), registration and filing fees under [File and Get Approved](/tools/this-years-figures#file-and-get-approved), your own exam, fingerprint and representative fees under [Qualify as an Adviser](/tools/this-years-figures#qualify-as-an-adviser), any bond or [minimum net worth](/glossary#minimum-net-worth) under [Risk and Insurance](/tools/this-years-figures#risk-and-insurance), and renewal under [Stay Registered and Compliant](/tools/this-years-figures#stay-registered-and-compliant).

## What you can count

Two figures, both already in your records, and both worth writing down exactly rather than rounding from memory:

- **Revenue by client.** A book concentrated in a few large relationships behaves very differently from one spread across many small ones when some of it does not follow you.
- **The recurring share.** Revenue that continues without new activity is the part a new firm can plan around. Everything else is a hope, and counting it here is the single most common way this estimate flatters itself.

Nothing else on this page is counted. From here down, every figure is an estimate you are choosing.

## What you have to estimate

The unknown that dominates the answer is how much of your book follows you, and there is no way to count it in advance. Estimate it as a band, not a number, and build the band from something harder than goodwill: how long each relationship has run, whether the client has ever moved an account before, and whether you or the firm's name is what they believe they hired.

What you may say to clients, and when, is governed by agreements you signed and is worked through in [Leave and Transition Clients](/process/leave-and-transition-clients). Read your own documents rather than assuming anything is standard, and treat this estimate as an estimate — testing it by asking a client is not a research method, it is the thing that agreement may prohibit.

Then run the band against costs. A worked example, with round hypothetical numbers: a book generating $240,000 a year, of which $180,000 recurs, against $120,000 of combined household and firm costs.

| If this much of the recurring book follows | Year-one revenue | Against $120,000 of costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 55% | $99,000 | Short by $21,000 |
| 70% | $126,000 | Clears by $6,000 |
| 85% | $153,000 | Clears by $33,000 |

The spread is the finding. A reader who takes the middle row as the answer has converted a range back into the single number step 1 warns against.

One more adjustment, and it is the one most often skipped: none of those figures arrive on day one. Accounts transfer over weeks, costs start when the entity does, and the gap between the two is what the runway actually has to cover.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are on the SEC path: your cost base is different from the one above, because a firm expecting assets at or above the threshold on [This year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#decide-if-independence-fits) does not pay a single home-state registration fee. It pays the SEC, plus a [notice filing](/glossary#notice-filing) fee in every state where it does enough business to require one — a line that grows with where clients live rather than staying fixed. Both are under [File and Get Approved](/tools/this-years-figures#file-and-get-approved).
</Callout>

## The result

One number: the months of household and firm costs you would need funded before the firm covers itself, taken at the **low** end of your band rather than the middle. Write it down with the portability range you used and what you based it on.

## What the number does and does not tell you

It tells you what the decision costs if the pessimistic case is the real one, which is the only version of it worth planning against.

It does not tell you whether to go. It says nothing about whether you want to own a business, which is the half of step 1 this sheet cannot reach, and nothing about whether the practice you would build is one you would want in five years. It is also only as good as the portability band, and that band is a judgement — a sheet cannot tell you that you have been optimistic, and everyone is.

## What to do with it

If the number is one you can fund, carry it into the written decision step 1 asks for: the go call, the range in months, and the assumptions under it. Later steps will ask you to revisit each of those, and the assumptions are what you will want back.

If it is not, that is an output, not a failure. Write the no down the same way, with the same figures — a decision you can reread is worth more than a decision you deferred. Then name what would have to change for the answer to flip: a larger recurring share, lower fixed costs, a longer funded runway, or a book that has had more time to settle. Date it, and set a point to run it again.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Four things make this estimate wrong, and none of them is arithmetic. Portability gets estimated from who is friendly rather than from who has actually moved an account before. Non-recurring revenue creeps back into the base because it feels unfair to exclude it. Costs get estimated for the firm while the household is left out, or the reverse. And the timing gets flattened — revenue that ramps over months gets compared against costs that start in full on day one. Rerun the sheet with only the pessimistic value for each of the four and see whether the answer survives; if it does, the range was real.
</Callout>
